# What Would YOUR Life Be Like in Victorian London?

Roteiro do vídeo 9 (~15 min narrados), **aguardando aprovação**. Cada linha numerada vira uma cena (uma frase, uma imagem).
Espinha: a vida de um vendedor de rua (costermonger) em Soho, Londres, em torno de 1854, contada em segunda pessoa ("you") por tema, não hora a hora: a cidade, o quarto, o dinheiro em pence e shillings, o trabalho (Mayhew), a comida e a névoa de carvão, a água, o surto de cólera da Broad Street e John Snow, transporte (ônibus a cavalo e o metrô de 1863), diversão e polícia, o Grande Fedor de 1858 e os esgotos de Bazalgette. Tese do fecho: a cidade só mudou rápido quando o fedor chegou ao Parlamento, e o conserto certo veio pela teoria errada (miasma). O personagem (vendedor, mulher e dois filhos) é fictício; o resto vem das fontes.
Os marcadores [F1], [F2]... apontam para a tabela de fontes no fim. **Checagem feita em 2026-10-12**, ver [../../pesquisa/checagem-v9-londres-vitoriana.md](../../pesquisa/checagem-v9-londres-vitoriana.md).

Cuidados de imagem: a cólera aparece só como pessoas deitadas e a bomba d'água, sem cadáveres nem sangue. Lampiões a gás, nada de luz elétrica. Jack, o Estripador, fica de fora (1888). Números, datas e a tabela de moedas ficam em cartões com pouco texto.


## 0. Abertura (gancho: o copo d'água)

1. Imagine you pour yourself a glass of water.
2. Now imagine you're in London, in the year 1854.
3. That water probably came out of the River Thames. [F20]
4. The same river that carries away the city's sewage. [F21]
5. Welcome to the biggest city on Earth. [F1]
6. It has the richest empire, the busiest port, and a very creative idea of plumbing.
7. Today we won't live as a lord, or as the Queen.
8. We'll live as you: a street seller in Soho, with a barrow, a wife, two kids, and one room.
9. You'll count pennies, meet a doctor with a map, and see a water pump that lost its handle.
10. And you'll live through a smell so bad that Parliament soaked its curtains in chemicals. [F41]
11. This is Victorian London.
12. Let's start with how big it was.

## 1. A cidade mais cheia do planeta

13. In the census of 1851, London counted more than two million people. [F1]
14. It was the biggest city on Earth, growing by roughly a fifth every ten years. [F1]
15. That same year, the city threw itself a party: the Great Exhibition. [F2]
16. A giant palace of iron and glass in Hyde Park, with about six million visits in five and a half months. [F2]
17. All those visitors needed a toilet, so the organizers built public flush toilets and charged one penny. [F3]
18. More than eight hundred thousand people paid. Some say that's where "spend a penny" comes from. [F3]
19. You may have heard that Victorians all died at forty.
20. That's a very misleading average, because so many babies died. [F40]
21. Survive childhood, and you had decent odds of reaching your fifties or sixties. [F40]
22. But where you lived, and how much money you had, changed the odds a lot. [F4]
23. In 1842, a civil servant named Edwin Chadwick compared towns across Britain. [F4]
24. In the same town, a labourer lived about twenty years less than a professional. [F4]
25. Money didn't just buy a nicer house. It bought years.
26. And you, my friend, are not a professional.

## 2. O seu quarto

27. Your home is one room in Soho, at the top of a narrow, creaky staircase.
28. You, your wife, and your two kids all sleep in here.
29. And that's the comfortable version.
30. Many poor Londoners couldn't afford a room, so they rented a bed by the night in a common lodging house.
31. A grown-up paid about threepence a night. A child, a penny and a half. [F7]
32. By the end of 1852, the police were inspecting more than three thousand of these houses. [F5]
33. Together, they held nearly fifty thousand people every night. [F5]
34. One writer in 1854 described a room in St Giles, less than fifteen feet square, with thirty-seven people on the floor. [F6]
35. Picture your living room. Now picture thirty-seven people in it.
36. Privacy was a hobby for rich people.
37. For water, you walk to the pump on the corner.
38. The streets outside are lit by gas lamps. Inside, you have a candle. [F38]
39. And for heat, you burn coal. Remember the coal. It's about to ruin the weather.

## 3. O dinheiro

40. Now let's talk money, because Victorian money was chaos.
41. Twelve pence make a shilling. Twenty shillings make a pound. [F8]
42. Yes, twelve, then twenty. Nobody asked for this.
43. For a very rough idea of today's value, multiply old prices by about one hundred and forty. [F9]
44. So a penny is roughly a dollar, and a shilling is roughly nine dollars. [F9]
45. Historians hate this trick, because food cost more back then, and gadgets cost less. [F9]
46. So what could a penny buy in 1854?
47. Four bunches of watercress. [F11]
48. A ticket to a penny gaff, a rowdy little theater. [F35]
49. Or, back in 1851, a visit to a flush toilet. [F3]
50. And a night's sleep in a lodging house? Threepence. [F7]
51. What did you earn? As a street seller, somewhere between ten and thirty shillings a week. [F10]
52. In January and February, closer to eight. [F10]
53. And if it rains, nobody buys your plums.

## 4. O trabalho (antes do sol)

54. Your day starts in the dark.
55. Around four or five in the morning, you walk to the market. [F11]
56. Covent Garden for fruit and vegetables. Billingsgate for fish. [F10]
57. You buy your stock, load the barrow, and spend the day shouting prices at strangers.
58. You're a costermonger, or "coster." A journalist named Henry Mayhew counted tens of thousands of you. [F10]
59. He did something unusual: he interviewed poor Londoners, and published their stories in a book called London Labour and the London Poor. [F10]
60. Costers even had a secret language, called back slang. You say words backward. [F12]
61. "Boy" becomes "yob." Yes, that yob. [F12]
62. Not every job was glamorous. Not even close.
63. Pure-finders collected dog droppings from the street, and sold them to tanneries to treat leather. [F13]
64. Yes. That was a job.
65. Mudlarks, many of them children, searched the muddy Thames shore for anything they could sell. [F14]
66. And toshers waded through the sewers, hunting for lost valuables. [F14]
67. Mayhew also met a girl who sold watercress. She said she was eight years old. [F11]
68. She went to the market between four and five every morning, and sold four bunches for a penny. [F11]
69. Most days she made three or four pence. A great day was one shilling and sixpence. [F11]
70. She told Mayhew: "I ain't a child, and I shan't be a woman till I'm twenty." [F11]
71. And about playing: "We children never play down there, 'cos we're thinking of our living." [F11]
72. Chimney sweeps used small boys to climb inside the chimneys. [F15]
73. A law banned it for anyone under twenty-one, in 1840. Many people simply ignored it. [F15]
74. More laws followed, and the practice only really ended after 1875. [F15]
75. And school wasn't compulsory until 1880. [F16]

## 5. O café da manhã e a névoa

76. Breakfast is bread and tea, maybe with butter, if it's a good week.
77. But even the food could be dodgy.
78. In the early 1850s, a doctor named Arthur Hassall studied food under the microscope. [F17]
79. He examined more than two and a half thousand samples for the medical journal The Lancet. [F17]
80. Many contained things that do not belong in food. [F17]
81. His reports helped lead to a food adulteration law in 1860. [F17]
82. Then you step outside, and the air is dodgy too.
83. London burns coal in kitchens, in factories, and in steam engines. [F18]
84. The smoke mixes with damp air from the Thames, and you get fog. [F18]
85. A thick yellow-brown fog that Londoners called a pea-souper. [F18]
86. Charles Dickens opened his novel Bleak House with it. [F18]
87. So that spooky foggy atmosphere is mostly coal smoke.

## 6. A água

88. Now, back to that glass of water.
89. In 1842, Chadwick argued that filth and bad drainage were killing the poor. [F21]
90. His plan: piped water, and sewers for every house. [F21]
91. But in 1854, many homes still used cesspools. Overflowing cesspools drained into sewers, and the sewers drained into the Thames. [F21]
92. And the Thames was also where water companies found their drinking water. [F20]
93. In July 1855, the scientist Michael Faraday took a steamboat on the river. [F19]
94. He dropped pieces of white card into the water to see how deep he could still see them. [F19]
95. They disappeared before sinking one inch. [F19]
96. He wrote to The Times that the whole river was "an opaque pale brown fluid." [F19]
97. The smell was bad. He called the river "for the time a real sewer." [F19]
98. A law from 1852 had ordered water companies to move their intakes upriver and filter the water. [F20]
99. But the deadlines were in 1855, so in 1854 some companies still drank from the dirty part of the river. [F20]
100. Remember that. A doctor was about to prove it.

## 7. Broad Street, 1854

101. In 1854, most doctors believed cholera spread through "miasma," meaning bad air from rotting things. [F24]
102. That's not crazy. Dirty places did smell, and dirty places did kill.
103. Cholera killed more than fourteen thousand Londoners in 1848 and 1849. [F23]
104. It came back in 1853 and 1854, and killed more than ten thousand more. [F23]
105. Now picture the end of August 1854. You live in Soho, near Broad Street.
106. On the thirty-first of August, people nearby start getting sick. [F25]
107. Within ten days, more than five hundred people die within about two hundred fifty yards of one street corner. [F25]
108. A doctor named John Snow thinks he knows why. [F26]
109. Snow is a teetotaler from a poor family in York. He has even given chloroform to Queen Victoria. [F26]
110. And he believes cholera travels in water, not in air. [F26]
111. So he talks to the families, and he marks the deaths on a map. [F25]
112. The dots cluster around one thing: the water pump on Broad Street. [F25]
113. Then the evidence gets stronger. A workhouse nearby has its own well. Out of five hundred thirty-five inmates, only five die. [F27]
114. A brewery on Broad Street employs over seventy workers who drink beer, not water. None gets seriously ill. [F27]
115. And a widow in Hampstead, miles away, has water from that pump delivered to her house by cart. [F28]
116. She dies of cholera on the second of September. So does her niece. [F28]
117. On the seventh of September, Snow convinces the local board to act. The next day, the pump handle is removed. [F29]
118. Cue the heroic music. But history is messier. [F29]
119. New cases were already falling, and many residents had fled. Snow himself admitted he couldn't be sure the handle made the difference. [F29]
120. The stronger proof came later, from a local curate, Reverend Henry Whitehead. [F30]
121. He started out sure that Snow was wrong. Then he investigated house by house. [F30]
122. At number forty Broad Street, a baby had died of cholera. Her family had emptied dirty water into a cesspool just a few feet from the pump's well. [F30]
123. That cesspool was leaking into the well. [F30]
124. Snow also compared two water companies. About three hundred thousand people had no say in which one they got. [F22]
125. The death rate with the dirtier water was many times higher. [F22]
126. Did the authorities listen? Not really. A Board of Health still blamed miasma. [F24]
127. Snow died in June 1858, aged forty-five, before most people agreed with him. [F31]
128. The Lancet's short obituary never mentioned his cholera work. It printed a correction in 2013. [F31]

## 8. Como se locomover

129. Getting around? Mostly, you walk.
130. If you have a few coins, you take an omnibus: a horse-drawn bus. [F32]
131. The first one in London ran in 1829, from Paddington to the Bank. [F32]
132. "Omnibus" is Latin for "for all."
133. Horses can only work a few hours a day, so every bus needs a small herd of them. [F32]
134. In 1855, the London General Omnibus Company was founded to bring order to the chaos. [F33]
135. Then, on the tenth of January 1863, London opened the world's first underground railway. [F34]
136. It ran from Paddington to Farringdon, and about thirty-eight thousand people rode it on opening day. [F34]
137. The trains were steam locomotives. In a tunnel. So yes, the stations and carriages filled with smoke. [F34]
138. Construction even had a plot twist: in 1862, the Fleet sewer burst and flooded the dig. [F34]

## 9. Diversão e crime (à noite)

139. Evening. You've earned a few pence, and you want some fun.
140. For a penny, you can enter a penny gaff. [F35]
141. That's a rowdy show in a pub back room or a small hall, with songs, clowns, and short plays about highwaymen and murderers. [F35]
142. Reformers panicked that it would corrupt the young. [F35]
143. If you prefer a proper hall, the Canterbury opened in Lambeth in 1852, one of London's first purpose-built music halls. [F36]
144. London's police are barely a generation old. Robert Peel created them in 1829. [F37]
145. By 1852, there were about fifty-six hundred officers for a city of more than two million. [F37]
146. Fagin is fiction, but you should still watch your pockets.
147. You've probably heard that Victorians covered piano legs, because bare legs were scandalous. [F39]
148. That seems to come from a joke in a travel book from 1839. A historian who looked for real examples found none. [F39]
149. And no, Jack the Ripper isn't here. That's 1888, decades after our story. [F46]

## 10. O Grande Fedor de 1858

150. Now fast-forward to the summer of 1858.
151. It's hot and dry, and the Thames is low. [F41]
152. Raw sewage sits on the riverbanks, baking in the sun. [F41]
153. And Parliament sits right on the river. [F41]
154. Lawmakers soak the curtains in chloride of lime to fight the smell. It doesn't work. [F41]
155. Some even talk about moving out of London. [F41]
156. Benjamin Disraeli calls the river "a Stygian pool, reeking with ineffable and intolerable horrors." [F42]
157. That's a very fancy way of saying "it stinks."
158. Faraday had sounded the alarm in 1855. Snow's map was four years old. Nothing much happened. [F47]
159. But a bad smell in Westminster? That moved Parliament fast. [F47]
160. In August 1858, a new law gave London three million pounds, repaid by a small tax on Londoners. [F42]
161. The engineer in charge was Joseph Bazalgette. [F43]
162. He built about eighty-two miles of main sewers, collecting from eleven hundred miles of street sewers. [F43]
163. It took three hundred and eighteen million bricks. [F43]
164. Sewage now flowed to pumping stations at the edge of the city, far from the drinking water. [F43]
165. One of them, Crossness, opened in 1865. The Prince of Wales started the engines. [F43]
166. Here's the funny part: Bazalgette designed it for the wrong theory, miasma. [F44]
167. But it worked anyway, because it kept sewage away from drinking water. [F44]
168. Right fix, wrong reason. A lot of progress happens like that.
169. Most of that system is still in use today. [F43]

## 11. Fecho: o preço do copo limpo

170. So, would you survive Victorian London?
171. Maybe. Even after the new sewers, one more big cholera outbreak hit the East End in 1866. [F45]
172. In total, over five thousand people died. It was traced to a water company's contaminated supply, in an area not yet connected to the new sewers. [F45]
173. Historians often call it the last major cholera outbreak in England. [F45]
174. Here's what stays with me.
175. London had Chadwick's report, Faraday's letter, and Snow's map. Three warnings.
176. But the poor of Soho, dying of dirty water, didn't change the city. The stink in Parliament did.
177. That's not a happy lesson. But it's a useful one.
178. Cities don't always change when people suffer. Sometimes they change when the suffering reaches the people in charge.
179. So the next time you flush a toilet, thank Joseph Bazalgette.
180. And look at that glass of water again. Clear, cold, boring.
181. That boring miracle took a doctor, a priest, a scientist, an engineer, and one truly awful smell.
182. If you enjoyed living in London for a while, tell me in the comments: which day in history should we live next?

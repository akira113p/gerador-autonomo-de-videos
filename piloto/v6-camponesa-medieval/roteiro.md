# What Would YOUR Life Be Like as a Medieval Peasant?

Proposta de roteiro (~15 min no ritmo dos vídeos anteriores), **aguardando aprovação**. Cada linha numerada vira uma cena (uma frase, uma imagem).
Espinha: um ano numa aldeia da Inglaterra por volta de 1300 (antes da Peste Negra), em segunda pessoa. O personagem "Hugh" é emprestado de um camponês real, Hugh Miller de Alwalton, listado nos Hundred Rolls de 1279; o resto (esposa, filhos, casa) é reconstrução geral. Segue o calendário agrícola (Michaelmas, inverno, Lady Day, colheita) e intercala temas: tribunal senhorial, dívidas ao senhor e à Igreja, mulheres e crianças, comida, o que se possuía. Desmente três mitos: "direito da primeira noite", "camponeses trabalhavam só 150 dias" e "todo mundo morria aos 30". Tese própria no fecho: o maior patrão do camponês era o clima e a margem apertada entre colheita e fome; foi preciso uma catástrofe (Peste Negra) para dar poder de barganha a quem sobreviveu.
Os marcadores [F1], [F2]... apontam para a tabela de fontes no fim. **Checagem feita em 2026-10-12**, ver [../../pesquisa/checagem-v6-camponesa-medieval.md](../../pesquisa/checagem-v6-camponesa-medieval.md).

Cuidados de imagem: sem sangue, sem corpos (a peste e a fome aparecem por símbolos: chuva, celeiro vazio, cartões). Objetos de época descritos pela forma (arado de bois, foice, tripé de panela de latão). Números e datas em cartões.

## 0. Abertura (gancho: o que o senhor leva)

1. Imagine a job where your boss takes three days of your week, unpaid. [F1]
2. And if your daughter wants to get married, you pay him a fee. [F6]
3. And when you die, he comes to your house and takes your best animal. [F6]
4. Welcome to England, around the year thirteen hundred.
5. You are a peasant, and this is your life.
6. Maybe you picture a muddy person, working every single day, and dead by thirty.
7. Some of that is true. A surprising amount of it is myth.
8. Did the lord really get to spend the wedding night with your bride? [F7]
9. Did peasants really work only a hundred and fifty days a year? [F22]
10. Today, we'll live one full year in an English village, from harvest to harvest.
11. We'll plough, sow, reap, pay, and get fined.
12. And at the end, we'll meet the disaster that hit your village hardest, and strangely helped your grandchildren. [F26] [F32]

## 1. Quem é você

13. First, you need a name. Let's call you Hugh.
14. And Hugh is not completely made up.
15. In 1279, King Edward the First sent officials around England to write down who held what. [F3]
16. In a village called Alwalton, they wrote down a man named Hugh Miller. [F1]
17. He was a villein, which means he held land from a lord, in return for work. [F6]
18. His holding was a virgate, which in Alwalton meant twenty-five acres. [F1]
19. That sounds like a nice big farm. It wasn't one piece.
20. Your land came as narrow strips, scattered across the village fields, mixed in with your neighbors' strips. [F31]
21. Hugh was in the middle of the pack. Alwalton had eighteen villeins with a full virgate, and five with half. [F2]
22. Below them, a crowd of cottagers, with a hut and a tiny plot. [F2]
23. England then had somewhere between four and a half and six million people. Historians still argue about it. [F4]
24. Most of them lived in the countryside, in villages like this one. [F5]
25. Above you sat the lord. In Alwalton, that was the abbot of Peterborough. [F1]
26. His estate was called a manor. And on a manor, the lord is landlord, boss, and judge, all at once. [F14]
27. Think of your village as a small company, where nobody asked you to sign the contract.

## 2. Michaelmas: o ano começa

28. Our year begins on the twenty-ninth of September, Michaelmas. [F10]
29. The harvest is in. The lord's officials are adding up the year's accounts. [F10]
30. The villagers choose a reeve, one of your own neighbors, to boss the farm work. [F10]
31. Today, Hugh also owes a payment called tallage. [F1]
32. The amount is whatever the abbot decides. [F1]
33. Now you can start ploughing for winter wheat. In your own fields, right?
34. Not so fast. First, you work for the lord.
35. Hugh owes the abbot three days of work, every single week of the year. [F1]
36. He gets exactly three weeks off, at Christmas, Easter, and Whitsun. [F1]
37. In autumn it gets worse. He must send two men a day. [F1]
38. On some manors, the lord at least feeds you during that work. [F33]
39. So you plough your own strips on the other days, whenever the weather and the oxen agree.
40. Ploughing is slow. Walter of Henley's handbook for estate managers says a team does three and a half roods a day. [F12]
41. A rood is a quarter of an acre. So, less than one acre a day. [F12]
42. Walter loves oxen. He says horses cost more to keep. [F13]
43. Then comes the seed. Walter says you need at least two bushels of wheat for every acre. [F12]
44. And he warns: if your harvest is only three times the seed, you gain nothing, unless grain sells well. [F12]
45. So farming is a bet. You throw grain on the ground, and hope the weather pays you back.
46. A prayer book made for a rich knight, around thirteen thirty, shows ploughing with oxen, harrowing, and the lord's mill. [F28]
47. It's one of the best pictures we have of ordinary people from this time, drawn in the margins of a book for the elite. [F28]

## 3. Inverno: a casa

48. By December, the fields are quiet. Time to go home.
49. Hugh's house is small, with one or two rooms. [F20]
50. In some regions, the animals live in the same building. [F20]
51. At Houndtor, on Dartmoor, archaeologists excavated a longhouse from the thirteenth century. [F20]
52. People and cattle came in through the same door. [F20]
53. The frame is wood. The walls are woven sticks plastered with mud. The roof is thatch. [F20]
54. In the middle of the floor, there's a fire. It almost never goes out. [F20]
55. There's no chimney. The smoke just drifts up and finds its way out through the roof. [F20]
56. Your house is also your smokehouse. Congratulations.
57. For a long time, historians thought houses like these fell apart within a generation. [F20]
58. But later medieval houses survive in their thousands, so many were sturdier than we imagined. [F20]
59. When the sun goes down, so does most of your day.
60. At Christmas, Hugh finally gets his week off from the abbot's work. [F1]
61. The church gives you more days off too. Sundays, and a long list of saints' days. [F22]
62. In winter, you thresh the grain, repair your tools, and mend your fences. Nothing glamorous.

## 4. Primavera: Lady Day e a fome

63. Spring begins with a strange date: the twenty-fifth of March, Lady Day. [F11]
64. In England, that was New Year's Day. The legal year started in March, not January. [F11]
65. And it stayed that way until seventeen fifty-two. [F11]
66. Now you sow the spring crops: barley, oats, and peas.
67. Then comes Lent, forty days without meat.
68. And here's a little secret of farming: spring is hungry.
69. Last year's grain is running out. This year's grain isn't ripe yet.
70. Cottagers feel it the hardest, with a tiny plot and no reserves.
71. At the time, people believed a farthing a day, the price of a loaf, could keep a person alive. [F34]
72. A farthing is a quarter of a penny.
73. Then Easter arrives, and Hugh's gift to the abbot is on the list. [F1]
74. Three hens, a rooster, and five eggs. [F1]
75. Happy Easter, from your landlord.

## 5. A mesa: pão, sopa e cerveja

76. So, what do you eat?
77. Mostly grain. Around thirteen hundred, up to eighty percent of a harvest worker's calories came from it. [F19]
78. Grain shows up in three ways: bread, pottage, and ale. [F19]
79. Bread is dark and heavy, often made from barley or rye, not fine white wheat. [F19]
80. Pottage is a thick stew, boiled with grain, peas, and vegetables, and maybe a scrap of bacon. [F19]
81. Ale is brewed from barley, and it spoils fast. So somebody is always brewing.
82. Who? Usually women. Historian Judith Bennett found that women brewed and sold most of England's ale. [F16]
83. Meat is rare. A fattened pig can give you bacon for the winter.
84. In the thirteenth century, harvest workers were given much bread, some cheese, and only small amounts of ale, fish, and meat. [F19]
85. And those workers ate better than most peasants. [F19]
86. So next time you complain about your sandwich, think of Hugh's lunch: bread, with a side of bread.

## 6. Verão e colheita

87. Then comes August, and the harvest.
88. This is the moment that decides your whole year.
89. Everybody helps. Wife, children, neighbors. All day, in the heat.
90. The grain is cut by hand, with a sickle, bent over, row after row.
91. And remember, the abbot gets two of your workers a day in autumn. [F1]
92. His grain gets cut first. Yours waits for the weather. [F1]
93. Then the sheaves are stacked, carried, threshed, and stored.
94. And now, the big myth. "Medieval peasants worked only a hundred and fifty days a year."
95. You've probably heard it. It spread from a popular book in the early nineteen-nineties, and it used the lowest numbers it could find. [F22]
96. Economist Gregory Clark looked again, and found that medieval farm workers worked at least about two hundred and fifty days a year. [F22]
97. Another study found that a farmer needed around three hundred days of work to feed a family before the Black Death. [F22]
98. So no. You do not have half the year off.
99. You get Sundays, a pile of saints' days, and three weeks free from the abbot. [F1] [F22]
100. If the weather was kind, you'll eat this winter. If not, keep watching until the end.

## 7. Dia de tribunal

101. Every three or four weeks, the whole village has to show up for court. [F14]
102. It's called the manor court. Think of it as a neighborhood meeting, where you can be fined.
103. The fines are called amercements. [F6]
104. And everything is written down, on parchment rolls. [F14]
105. Some of these rolls survive. We can read what people actually got fined for. [F15]
106. In Morden, near London, in October thirteen ninety-nine, the court's ale-tasters reported brewers who broke the assize. [F15]
107. Their punishment was two pence per brew. [F15]
108. Others were fined for breaking the lord's hedges, or for trespassing on a neighbor's land. [F15]
109. In 1406, one man simply left the village. The court seized his empty land for the lord. [F15]
110. Judith Bennett found that this ale fine worked more like a licence. Most brewers were fined, whether they cheated or not. [F16]
111. So the "fine" was basically the price of a permit.
112. And who judged all this? Mostly tenants. People like you. [F14]
113. So when you complain about the lord's court, remember that your neighbors help run it.
114. And if you hate it all, you can run. Some villeins fled to towns. [F18]
115. A custom in many towns said that if a villein lived there for a year and a day, he became free. [F18]
116. But he had to stay hidden, and not be dragged back, for that whole year. [F18]

## 8. As contas

117. Let's add up Hugh's bills.
118. To the lord: three days of work a week, tallage, hens, and eggs. [F1]
119. If your daughter marries, you pay merchet. [F6]
120. Now, about that myth. Yes, merchet was about marriage. No, it wasn't what the movies say.
121. Historians have found no evidence that a "right of the first night" ever existed as a law in medieval Europe. [F7]
122. Merchet was a fine, a payment to the lord, perhaps for losing a worker. [F7]
123. Less movie, more tax form.
124. When you die, your family owes the lord a heriot. [F6]
125. That's usually your best animal. [F6]
126. Then the church takes the second best. That's called a mortuary. [F8]
127. Your family has just lost a person, and also two animals.
128. And all year, the church takes a tenth of what you grow. That's the tithe. [F9]
129. A tenth of your grain, paid in grain. [F9]
130. Do the math. Half of Hugh's working week goes to the abbot. [F1]
131. Plus a tenth of his harvest to the church. [F9]
132. And whatever is left has to feed a family, and pay for seed.

## 9. Alice, as crianças e a escola

133. Hugh isn't doing this alone. Let's give him a wife. Her name is Alice.
134. Alice works the fields, cooks, raises the children, and probably brews the ale. [F16]
135. Everybody works, children too.
136. A coroner's record mentions a ten-year-old boy hurt while chopping vegetables in a manor kitchen. [F29]
137. Historian Jeremy Goldberg estimated that one in ten rural people over fourteen worked as a servant. [F29]
138. Now, school. Can your son learn to read? Maybe. But not for free.
139. A villein needed the lord's permission to send a child to school. [F17]
140. In 1339, in Winslow, Richard Ponteys was fined three pence for sending his son Geoffrey to school without a licence. [F17]
141. Three pence for a school without paperwork. Bureaucracy is older than you think.
142. Some lords did give permission. At Norton, there were seventeen licences between 1300 and 1348. [F17]
143. Why would a lord care? My guess: a schooled son is a worker who might leave.

## 10. Quanto você vive, e o que você tem

144. So, how long do you live?
145. You've heard that people died at thirty.
146. That's a statistic about birth, not about adults.
147. Estimates for landholding families put life expectancy at birth around thirty-one. [F23]
148. But so many children died young that they dragged the average down.
149. Those who survived childhood often reached middle age, and beyond. [F23]
150. Not that you'd be in good shape. No antibiotics, no vaccines, and not much help when you got sick.
151. And what do you actually own?
152. Here's a real list. In the thirteen-forties, a coroner's roll in Derbyshire listed the goods of a man named John Fox. [F21]
153. A house and ten acres. A mare and her colt. Some cattle, sheep, and a pig. [F21]
154. A brass pot. A pan. Two chests. And some oats. [F21]
155. That's a man with land.
156. Historians warn that lists like this leave things out. [F21]
157. But the picture is clear. Your net worth is a house, some land, and a pot.

## 11. A chuva

158. So, how does Hugh's year end?
159. If he's lucky, with enough grain. If he's unlucky, like in 1315, it starts with rain.
160. In the spring of 1315, heavy rain began in England, and it kept falling. [F24]
161. The grain couldn't ripen. The hay couldn't dry. Food prices shot up. [F24]
162. The harvest failed in 1315, and again in 1316. [F24]
163. People ate the seed grain. And a smaller harvest came next year. [F24]
164. A few years later, a cattle plague killed more than half of England's cattle, by one estimate. [F25]
165. Your oxen were your tractors. So that hurts twice.
166. Then, in 1348, the Black Death arrives. [F26]
167. Historians estimate that a third to a half of the English died. [F26]
168. Villages like Alwalton would have lost many of their neighbors.
169. And then comes the part nobody expects. Suddenly, there were too few workers and plenty of land. [F32]
170. Lords needed workers. Workers wanted more pay. Wages rose. [F32]
171. In 1351, Parliament passed the Statute of Labourers: wages must stay at pre-plague levels. [F27]
172. It largely failed. [F27]
173. Over time, lords turned work into cash rent, and villeinage faded. [F32]
174. And in 1381, peasants marched on London, furious about a new poll tax. [F30]
175. Here's my take. In 1300, the lord was not your biggest boss. The weather was.
176. Too many people, too little land, and rain you couldn't argue with.
177. The weak spot of the Middle Ages wasn't kings. It was the thin margin between a harvest and a famine.
178. And it took a catastrophe to finally give people like Hugh some leverage. [F32]
179. That's a grim kind of progress.
180. Hugh never saw it. But his grandchildren might have.
181. So next time you complain about taxes, remember Hugh, who gave half his working week to the abbot.
182. If you enjoyed this year in the countryside, tell me in the comments: which life in history should we live next?

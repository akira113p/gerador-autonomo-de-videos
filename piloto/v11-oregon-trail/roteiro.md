# What Would YOUR Life Be Like on the Oregon Trail?

Roteiro do vídeo 11 (~183 cenas, ~2083 palavras, cerca de 14 a 15 min). Cada linha numerada vira uma cena (uma frase, uma imagem).
Espinha: você (segunda pessoa) sai de Independence, Missouri, em abril de 1850 com uma carroça coberta rumo ao Oregon: o que comprar e quanto custava, o dia na estrada, rios, cólera (maior causa de morte), o mito do ataque indígena, os povos que já viviam ali, a travessia final (Barlow Road) e a chegada. O personagem é o espectador; a única pessoa real seguida com detalhe é Amelia Knight (diário de 1853). Tese do fecho: a trilha foi uma caminhada longa e lenta de gente comum, em que o perigo era um balde de água e não uma flecha, e a "terra vazia" do fim era a casa de outros povos.
Os marcadores [F1], [F2]... apontam para a tabela de fontes no fim. **Checagem feita em 2026-10-12**, ver [../../pesquisa/checagem-v11-oregon-trail.md](../../pesquisa/checagem-v11-oregon-trail.md).

Cuidados de imagem: sem pessoas indígenas desenhadas (cartões, mãos e paisagens); sem violência, sangue ou corpos (cólera e Donner só por cartões e túmulos de longe); a carroça é descrita pela forma. Mapas e números em cartão, com poucas palavras.

## 0. Abertura (gancho: você não vai de carroça, vai a pé)
1. It's April, eighteen fifty. [F1]
2. You're standing in a muddy street in Independence, Missouri. [F1]
3. Behind you is a wagon packed to the roof.
4. In front of you are two thousand miles of Oregon Trail. [F2]
5. No highway. No gas stations. No bathrooms.
6. Welcome to the least glamorous road trip in history.
7. And here is the part the cartoons skip: you will not be riding. [F3]
8. You'll be walking beside that wagon for four to six months. [F2] [F3]
9. That's roughly four million steps, give or take a blister. [F2]
10. Some people did it for land. Some, for gold. Some, for a better life. [F4] [F7]
11. And by one estimate, at least twenty thousand people never arrived. [F5]
12. You probably think you know what killed them. You're probably wrong.
13. So let's pack the wagon and find out.

## 1. Por que alguém faria isso?
14. First question: why would a sane person do this?
15. Start with the sales pitch. Free farmland in a green valley called the Willamette. [F6]
16. This very year, Congress will promise a married couple six hundred and forty acres. [F6]
17. A single man gets half of that. [F6]
18. Now, that valley was not empty. The Kalapuya people lived there. [F30]
19. Nobody asked them, and a treaty with them would not be signed until eighteen fifty-five. [F30]
20. The brochures leave that part out.
21. Then there's California, where gold has just been found. [F7]
22. In eighteen forty-nine, about thirty thousand people headed west, mostly for California. [F7]
23. In eighteen fifty, by one count, it was fifty-five thousand. [F7]
24. So you won't be alone. You're joining the slowest traffic jam in American history.
25. Over the whole era, historians guess that hundreds of thousands took this road. [F28]
26. Why so vague? Because nobody was counting at a toll booth.
27. For comparison, today you would rent a truck and call it a long weekend.

## 2. Dia de compras (a carroça e os bois)
28. Now, shopping day. And it is not cheap.
29. A proper outfit costs roughly eight hundred to twelve hundred dollars. [F10]
30. That includes a year of supplies, because you can't grow a crop on the way. [F10]
31. In today's money, that's more than thirty thousand dollars. [F10]
32. First, the wagon. Forget the cartoons.
33. The bed is about ten feet long and four feet wide. [F8]
34. Over it, a canvas cover stretched on five or six curved wooden hoops. [F9]
35. Your entire life has to fit in something the size of a big closet.
36. And no, this is not a Conestoga wagon. [F9]
37. The Conestoga was heavy, with a swayback shape, and it rarely went past the Missouri River. [F9]
38. Pioneers used a lighter wagon with straight sides. The nickname was "prairie schooner." [F9]
39. Now the engine. Oxen, or mules?
40. Mules are faster. Oxen pull more, and they're less likely to stampede. [F11]
41. At Fort Laramie in eighteen fifty, someone counted over thirty-six thousand oxen, and about seventy-five hundred mules. [F11]
42. A few years later, Army captain Randolph Marcy priced an eight-ox team at about two hundred dollars. [F11]
43. For two thousand miles, he says, young oxen will outlast mules. [F11]
44. The lesson of the trail, so far: the fastest option is rarely the one that arrives.
45. Nobody is selling extended warranties out here.

## 3. O que vai na carroça
46. Now, food. And here we get help from a classic manual, Marcy's The Prairie Traveler. [F12]
47. It came out in eighteen fifty-nine, so you're a little early. But the advice holds up. [F12]
48. His list, per grown person, for about a hundred and ten days: [F12]
49. One hundred and fifty pounds of flour. [F12]
50. Twenty-five pounds of bacon. [F12]
51. Fifteen pounds of coffee, and twenty-five of sugar. [F12]
52. That's the menu. Bread, bacon, coffee. For months.
53. Plus some cattle that walk along with you, and are also lunch. [F12]
54. Everything else is a gamble. How many shovels? How many spare wheels?
55. And remember, every pound you pack, an ox has to pull.
56. A wagon like yours can carry up to about twenty-five hundred pounds, in theory. [F8]
57. In practice, travelers aimed for sixteen to eighteen hundred. [F8]
58. So the rocking chair, the mirror, and the heavy furniture all have to go. Or go later.
59. Many families did dump things along the road, to save their animals. [F18]
60. One pioneer woman, Amelia Knight, threw out jars, buckets, and all her pickles. [F18]
61. It's a hard truth of the trail: when you're an ox, a pickle is a very heavy thing.

## 4. O problema do guia (Hastings e Donner)
62. Then there's the guidebook problem.
63. One famous book was The Emigrants' Guide to Oregon and California, by Lansford Hastings. [F13]
64. It was published in eighteen forty-five. [F13]
65. Hastings told readers to bring a good gun. Fair enough. [F13]
66. He also described a shortcut across the desert west of the Great Salt Lake. [F13]
67. That description was only a few sentences long. [F13]
68. And Hastings had never actually traveled that route. [F13]
69. In eighteen forty-six, the Donner Party tried it. [F14]
70. Snow trapped them in the Sierra Nevada. Of eighty-seven people, about forty-eight survived. [F14]
71. It's the darkest story of the trail, and we won't dwell on it.
72. The lesson is smaller, and more useful: check whether the author has actually been there.
73. Which, by the way, is also a good rule for YouTube videos.

## 5. Um dia na estrada
74. Now, the trail itself. Your alarm clock is a trumpet, or a rifle shot, around four in the morning. [F15]
75. Breakfast. Round up the animals. Hitch the wagons. [F15]
76. At about seven, the wagon master shouts, "Wagons roll!" [F15]
77. And then you walk. [F3]
78. The average is twelve to fifteen miles a day. [F15]
79. That's about a half marathon, every day, for months, while herding cattle. [F15]
80. At midday, there's a one-hour break called "nooning." [F15]
81. Around six, the wagons form a circle. [F15]
82. That circle is mostly a corral for the animals. [F15]
83. Out on the plains, there are almost no trees. [F16]
84. So what do you burn for dinner? Dried buffalo dung. They called it "buffalo chips." [F16]
85. Someone has to collect it. Usually, women and children. [F16]
86. Dinner: bacon, bread, and coffee, cooked over burning dung. [F12] [F16]
87. Historian Lillian Schlissel studied the diaries and letters of one hundred and three women. [F29]
88. They cook, they drive oxen, they care for children, and some give birth, all on the road. [F29]
89. Amelia Knight wrote on April twenty-third: "Dreary times, wet and muddy." [F18]
90. She was pregnant with her eighth child, who was born days after the family arrived. [F18]
91. Not exactly a vacation.
92. There is no phone to scroll. Your screen time is the horizon, and the back of an ox.

## 6. Os rios
93. Then come the rivers.
94. Few of the big rivers have a bridge. [F17]
95. Sometimes you float the wagon across on a makeshift raft. [F17]
96. Sometimes you pay a ferry. In eighteen fifty, one party paid four dollars a wagon on the Platte. [F17]
97. At one crossing, Knight counted more than three hundred wagons waiting their turn. [F17]
98. Her group built a ferry out of rope and wagon beds. [F17]
99. The women and children crossed last. [F17]
100. On a steamboat, one of her oxen jumped overboard and swam across by itself. [F18]
101. It was the only one with a plan.
102. But rivers were no joke. Drowning at river crossings was a real cause of death. [F17]
103. And in summer, the opposite problem: water holes dried up, and oxen died. [F31]
104. Remember that word: water.
105. Marcy, years later, would advise boiling water from stagnant pools. [F12]
106. Good advice. Whether anyone followed it is another question.

## 7. A doença (cólera)
107. Because the biggest killer on the trail was not a person.
108. It was cholera. [F5]
109. Cholera is a bacterium. It spreads through water and food contaminated by human waste. [F19]
110. Now picture a campsite with thousands of strangers, one creek, and no toilets. [F19]
111. In those early gold rush years, it struck the outfitting towns every spring. [F19]
112. Then it followed the trail, campground by campground. [F19]
113. You could be healthy in the morning and dead by noon. [F19]
114. Nobody knew what caused it. People treated it with camphor and laudanum. [F19]
115. One emigrant counted seventy-one graves at a single campground. [F19]
116. In eighteen fifty, by one count, cholera killed thousands. [F7]
117. The Bureau of Land Management says at least twenty thousand people died on the Oregon Trail. [F5]
118. Cholera, accidents, drownings, and other illnesses combined. [F5]
119. Estimates vary a lot, so treat any number as a ballpark. [F5]
120. Most of those graves are unmarked. [F5]
121. One historian, John Unruh, even argued that the trail was probably not much deadlier than staying home. [F32]
122. Because in those same years, cholera was killing thousands in cities like Saint Louis. [F32]
123. The most dangerous thing on the trail wasn't a person. It was a bucket of water.

## 8. O mito do ataque
124. Now, the scene you were waiting for. Wagons in a circle, arrows in the air.
125. Let's check the numbers.
126. Unruh counted, between eighteen forty and eighteen sixty, three hundred and sixty-two emigrants killed in clashes with Native people. [F20]
127. And four hundred and twenty-six Native people killed in the same clashes. [F20]
128. Over twenty years. Along a road used by hundreds of thousands. [F20] [F28]
129. Those figures are tentative, and probably low. But they're not thousands. [F20]
130. Compare that with cholera. [F5]
131. Even the National Park Service says wagon trains were attacked far less often than the movies suggest. [F20]
132. And most encounters, says the Park Service, were peaceful. [F20]
133. And Unruh concluded that more Native people were killed by emigrants than the other way around. [F20]
134. Many emigrants began the trip in needless fear, and sometimes fired first. [F20]
135. Amelia Knight's daughters hid in the wagon the first time they saw Native people. [F18]
136. By May second, she wrote, "Children are getting used to them." [F18]
137. Still, the fear was real, and it shaped the whole trip.
138. So the movie got one thing right: there was danger. It just got the proportions wrong.

## 9. Os povos que já estavam lá
139. But the trail was not empty land.
140. It crossed the homelands of many nations.
141. Many Native people helped. They ran ferries, offered food, and guided travelers across dangerous rivers. [F21]
142. Sometimes for payment, sometimes for free. [F21]
143. They traded, too. They wanted livestock, clothing, metal tools, and mirrors. [F21]
144. But a wagon train is not a quiet guest.
145. Emigrant livestock stripped the grass for several miles on each side of the trail. [F22]
146. The traffic disrupted the buffalo migrations that Plains peoples depended on. [F22]
147. And cholera followed the trail into Native camps and villages. [F22]
148. In September eighteen fifty-one, the United States held a council at Horse Creek, east of Fort Laramie. [F23]
149. The goal was to legalize emigrant passage through Native lands, and reduce violence. [F23]
150. The United States promised yearly payments for fifty years. As ratified, Congress cut that to ten. [F23]
151. So when you picture the trail from the wagon seat, remember there was a very different view from the other side.
152. Their story did not end where the wagon trail did.

## 10. Montanhas e o fim da estrada
153. By midsummer, you reach Independence Rock, in Wyoming. [F25]
154. A priest called it the "Great Register of the Desert," because of all the names carved there. [F25]
155. Today, there are more than five thousand names. [F25]
156. Next comes South Pass, the easy way over the Rockies. [F24]
157. It's a valley about twelve miles wide. A wagon can cross it without any climbing drama. [F24]
158. Then comes the hard part: dry stretches, tired oxen, and the Columbia River still ahead. [F31]
159. Near the end, you face a choice.
160. Float down the Columbia River by boat, or take the Barlow Road around Mount Hood. [F26]
161. Until the Barlow Road opened in eighteen forty-six, the overland trail effectively ended at a town called The Dalles. [F26]
162. The Barlow Road is a toll road. Five dollars per wagon, plus ten cents per head of livestock. [F26]
163. It includes Laurel Hill, a descent of roughly two thousand feet. [F26]
164. Wagons were chained by the wheels and lowered with ropes. [F26]
165. Over the years, the ropes wore chutes in the hill as deep as seven feet. [F26]
166. It was a one-way road, with a toll booth and a very steep hill. [F26]

## 11. Chegada
167. The trail officially ends at Oregon City, Oregon. [F2]
168. But let's finish with Amelia Knight, in eighteen fifty-three. [F18]
169. Her diary ends with these words: "This is the journey's end." [F18]
170. Her husband traded two yoke of oxen for half a section of land, and a small log cabin. [F18]
171. That's three hundred and twenty acres, for two yoke of oxen. [F18]
172. Her eighth child was born just days after they arrived. [F18]
173. In eighteen sixty-nine, the railroad changed everything. [F27]
174. An emigrant could go from Omaha to the Pacific in less than two weeks. [F27]
175. The wagon trail faded. But about three hundred miles of ruts are still there. [F27]
176. So what was the Oregon Trail, really?
177. Not a western movie. A long, slow commute, on foot, by ordinary people.
178. Where the most dangerous thing was a bucket of water, not an arrow.
179. And where the "empty land" at the end was somebody's home.
180. For many settlers, the gamble paid off. For the people already there, it was the start of a very hard chapter.
181. Which brings us back to that muddy street in Independence.
182. Would you walk four million steps for a farm in someone else's valley?
183. Tell me in the comments: what's the first thing you'd leave behind?

# What Did Ancient Romans Actually Do All Day?

Vídeo 2 do canal (~20 min). Aprovado pelo usuário em 2026-10-10. Cada linha numerada vira uma cena (uma frase, uma imagem).
Espinha do roteiro: o poema 4.8 de Martial (c. 88 d.C.), que descreve o dia romano hora por hora.

## 0. Abertura (gancho: o presente)

1. Your alarm goes off at six thirty.
2. You check your phone, and your whole day is already cut into little boxes.
3. Meetings. Deadlines. Lunch at twelve sharp.
4. Now rewind about nineteen hundred years, to the biggest city in the Western world.
5. Rome, around the year 90 AD. Somewhere between half a million and a million people. Nobody knows exactly. [F1]
6. Nobody there owns a clock that ticks.
7. And yet a Roman poet left us something remarkable: a schedule.
8. In a short poem, the poet Martial laid out how a day in Rome was spent, hour by hour. [F2]
9. So let's borrow his schedule, and live one ordinary day in ancient Rome.
10. From the first hour, to the last.

## 1. O que era uma hora em Roma

11. But first, a problem. What exactly is an hour in Rome?
12. For the Romans, daytime ran from sunrise to sunset, and it was always divided into twelve hours. [F3]
13. Always twelve. No matter the season.
14. So in June, when days are long, one Roman hour lasted about seventy-five of our minutes. [F3]
15. In December, it shrank to about forty-five.
16. Your lunch break literally got shorter in winter.
17. To tell the time, Romans looked at the sun.
18. And for a long time, they did it badly.
19. According to Pliny the Elder, the first public sundial in the Roman Forum was war loot, brought back from Catania, in Sicily, in 263 BC. [F4]
20. There was one small problem. Catania is much further south than Rome.
21. So the lines on the dial didn't match the hours in Rome.
22. Pliny says the Romans kept following that wrong sundial for ninety-nine years. [F4]
23. And not everyone loved sundials, even the accurate ones.
24. In an old Roman comedy, a hungry character curses the man who invented hours. [F5]
25. "May the gods destroy the man who first discovered the hours," he says, "and who set up a sundial here, and chopped my day into little pieces." [F5]
26. When he was a boy, he says, his belly was his sundial.
27. He ate whenever he was hungry. Now he eats when the sun allows it.
28. In 159 BC, Rome finally got a water clock, which measured the hours with flowing water, even at night and on cloudy days. [F4]
29. But most people simply looked at the sky, and listened to the city.
30. And the city was loud.

## 2. Acordar numa insula (antes da primeira hora)

31. Our Roman wakes up before sunrise, in a rented room on the third floor of an apartment block.
32. The Romans called these blocks insulae: islands.
33. They were tall, crowded, and often badly built.
34. According to the geographer Strabo, Emperor Augustus limited their height to seventy feet, about twenty-one meters. [F6]
35. The satirist Juvenal joked that Rome was a city held up by thin wooden props. [F7]
36. Collapses and fires were a constant fear. [F7]
37. But the everyday enemy was noise.
38. By law, most wagons were banned from the city streets from sunrise until the last two hours of daylight. [F8]
39. So deliveries rolled through the narrow streets at night.
40. Juvenal complains that in Rome, a good night's sleep costs a fortune. [F7]
41. And that many sick people in the city die simply from lack of sleep. [F7]
42. There is no running water in our Roman's room.
43. Water comes from a public fountain down the street, carried up the stairs in a jar.
44. And the toilet? For many people, a chamber pot. Or a walk to a public latrine.
45. Getting dressed is quick: a simple wool tunic, belted at the waist.
46. If he is a citizen with business to do, he also puts on a toga.
47. A huge, heavy woolen sheet, very hard to drape properly without help.
48. Then he steps out into the street, and into the crowd.
49. Juvenal describes it: a wave of people in front, a mass of people pushing from behind. [F30]
50. One man jabs you with his elbow, another with a pole. Someone bangs a beam into your head, someone else a wine jar. [F30]
51. Your legs are caked with mud, big feet tread on you from every side, and a soldier's hobnail sticks into your toe. [F30]
52. On his way, he passes the fountain, full of water that has traveled for miles.
53. Rome was fed by aqueducts, channels that brought fresh water from the hills, mostly underground and on huge arches. [F31]
54. The man in charge of them, Frontinus, was very proud of his job. [F31]
55. He wrote: compare these, if you will, with the idle pyramids, or the useless, though famous, works of the Greeks. [F31]
56. Roman engineers were not modest people.

## 3. Café da manhã e escola

57. Breakfast was small: some bread, perhaps with cheese, olives or a little salt.
58. And many people simply bought it on the street.
59. Martial wrote a two-line poem about it. [F9]
60. "Get up! The baker is already selling breakfast to the boys, and the crested birds of dawn are singing everywhere." [F9]
61. Those boys were on their way to school.
62. And school started early. Very early.
63. Martial lived near a schoolmaster, and he hated him. [F10]
64. "The crested roosters haven't even broken the silence," he wrote, "and you're already thundering with savage shouting and beatings." [F10]
65. Lessons often happened in a shop or under a porch, open to the street.
66. So the whole neighborhood heard every mistake.

## 4. Primeira e segunda hora: a salutatio

67. Now the first hour begins. And Martial tells us exactly what happens.
68. "The first and second hour wear out the people paying their morning calls." [F2]
69. This was the salutatio, the morning greeting.
70. Rome ran on a system of patrons and clients.
71. A rich patron protected his clients, and helped them with money, jobs and legal trouble.
72. In return, the clients gave him something every rich Roman wanted: a crowd.
73. So every morning, clients in their togas lined up at his door to wish him good morning.
74. The bigger the crowd at your door, the more important you looked.
75. The clients were often rewarded with a small handout, called the sportula: the little basket. [F11]
76. Originally it was food. By Martial's time, it was usually cash. [F11]
77. Martial and Juvenal both name the amount: a hundred quadrantes. [F11]
78. The quadrans was the smallest copper coin in Rome. Keep it in mind. It will come back later.
79. After the greeting, the clients might follow their patron to the Forum, like a walking advertisement.
80. Martial spent years doing this, and complained about it constantly. [F11]

## 5. Terceira hora: os tribunais

81. "The third hour," says Martial, "keeps the hoarse lawyers busy." [F2]
82. Trials were held in the open, in the forums and in the great basilicas.
83. And the speeches were timed. With water clocks.
84. Pliny the Younger, a lawyer and senator, wrote that in one big trial before the Senate he spoke for almost five hours. [F12]
85. He had been given twelve water clocks of speaking time, and four more were added. [F12]
86. Big trials drew crowds, like a show.
87. Pliny also complained that some speakers hired people to sit in the audience and applaud. [F13]
88. Paid applause. Two thousand years before the laugh track.

## 6. Até a quinta hora: o trabalho

89. "Until the fifth hour," Martial continues, "Rome stretches out her many kinds of work." [F2]
90. And most Romans did work. Hard.
91. The city was full of tabernae: small shops opening straight onto the street.
92. Often, the family that ran the shop lived in a room above it or behind it.
93. Bakers, butchers, shoemakers, barbers, blacksmiths, all side by side.
94. The barber's shop was a place to sit, shave and catch up on the gossip.
95. But Roman barbers had no fine steel safety razors, and some were famously slow.
96. Martial wrote about one barber who worked so slowly that, by the time he'd gone round his client's face, a new beard had grown. [F32]
97. Behind much of this work were people who never appear in Martial's schedule.
98. Many of the cooks, porters, bath attendants and shop workers of Rome were enslaved.
99. Their day ended when someone else decided it did.
100. Some of the busiest workers were the fullers, who washed and finished wool cloth.
101. Their secret ingredient was urine. [F14]
102. It was collected in jars, then workers standing in tubs stomped it into the cloth with their feet. [F14]
103. Urine was so valuable that Emperor Vespasian put a tax on it. [F15]
104. His son Titus found this disgusting.
105. So, according to the historian Suetonius, Vespasian held a coin from the first payment under his son's nose, and asked if it smelled. [F15]
106. Titus said no.
107. "And yet," said Vespasian, "it comes from urine." [F15]
108. Lunch was quick and light, often eaten standing up.
109. And for that, there were street bars.
110. In Pompeii, a small town south of Rome buried by Vesuvius in 79 AD, archaeologists have found many of these counters. [F16]
111. In 2020, they finished excavating one that still had its bright paintings. [F16]
112. Ducks, a rooster, a dog, and a sea nymph riding a sea horse. [F16]
113. In the jars set into the counter, they found the remains of duck, pig, goat, fish and snails. [F16]
114. Fast food, nearly two thousand years ago.
115. And on almost everything, Romans poured garum: a sauce made from fermented fish. [F17]
116. Pliny the Elder describes it as fish guts and leftover parts, soaked in salt until they turned to liquid. [F17]
117. Sounds terrible. But modern fish sauce is made in a very similar way, and you might have some in your kitchen.
118. And not every morning was the same.
119. Their official calendar didn't run on our seven-day week. It ran on a market cycle of eight days. [F33]
120. Every eighth day was the nundinae, market day. [F33]
121. Farmers came into town to sell their produce, and to hear about new laws. [F33]
122. Instead of Monday, the Romans had market day.

## 7. Sexta hora: o descanso

123. "The sixth hour," says Martial, "is rest for the tired." [F2]
124. Around midday, the city slowed down. People ate, and many took a nap.
125. And that habit left a mark that still exists today.
126. The sixth hour, in Latin, is sexta hora.
127. In Spanish, it became siesta. [F18]

## 8. Sétima hora: fim do trabalho e os muros que falam

128. "The seventh," Martial says, "will be the end." [F2]
129. For many Romans, the working day was over by early afternoon.
130. Time to walk around, meet friends, and read the walls.
131. Because Roman walls talked.
132. In Pompeii alone, archaeologists have recorded thousands of painted notices and scratched graffiti. [F19]
133. There were election posters, painted in big red letters.
134. One candidate, Marcus Cerrinius Vatia, was supported by "all the late drinkers." [F20]
135. Another poster for him came from "the little thieves." [F20]
136. Some historians suspect his rivals painted those ones. [F20]
137. There were visitors leaving their mark. [F21]
138. "Gaius Pumidius Dipilus was here," dated to the third of October, 78 BC. [F21]
139. There was a very honest review, scratched on a wall by a doorway, and addressed to the host. [F22]
140. "We wet the bed, host. I admit it, we did wrong. If you want to know why: there was no chamber pot." [F22]
141. And there was one graffito, found in several places in Pompeii, that says it all. [F23]
142. "I'm amazed, O wall, that you haven't collapsed under the weight of so many boring writers." [F23]

## 9. Oitava e nona hora: as termas

143. "The eighth hour, until the ninth, belongs to the oiled-up wrestling grounds," says Martial. [F2]
144. In other words: it's bath time.
145. The public baths were the heart of Roman social life.
146. And they were cheap. Entry could cost a single quadrans. [F24]
147. Remember the hundred coins from the morning handout? One of them buys you a bath.
148. First, exercise: running, wrestling, ball games, lifting weights.
149. Then the rooms: the warm room, the hot room, and the cold pool.
150. And who went to the baths? Almost everyone. Men and women.
151. In many baths, they went at different times.
152. A bronze tablet from a Roman mining town in what is now Portugal lists the rules of its baths. [F34]
153. Women bathe from the first hour to the seventh. Men from the eighth hour until two hours after dark. [F34]
154. And the price? Men paid half an as. Women paid a whole as. Double. [F34]
155. There was no soap as we know it.
156. Romans covered themselves in oil and scraped it off with a curved metal tool called a strigil.
157. Oil, sweat and dirt, all in one go.
158. And the baths were loud.
159. We know exactly how loud, because the philosopher Seneca once lived right above one. [F25]
160. He described the noise in a letter. [F25]
161. The grunts of men lifting lead weights.
162. The slap of the masseur's hands on someone's shoulders.
163. A ball player counting his score out loud.
164. The man who sings in the bath, because he loves the sound of his own voice.
165. The splash of people jumping into the pool.
166. The hair-plucker, shrieking to attract customers, and only going quiet when he made someone else scream instead.
167. And the sellers of sausages, pastries and drinks, each with his own special cry.
168. Seneca insisted that a true philosopher could ignore all of this.
169. And then, at the end of the very same letter, he admits he's moving out. [F25]
170. The baths also had public latrines: long rows of stone seats, side by side, with no walls between them.
171. Instead of toilet paper, Seneca mentions a sponge on a stick, kept in the latrine for wiping. [F26]
172. Quite possibly shared.

## 10. Nona e décima hora: o jantar

173. "The ninth hour," Martial writes, "orders us to crush the piled-up couches." [F2]
174. Dinner time. The cena, the main meal of the day.
175. In wealthy homes, guests lay on couches arranged around a low table, three to a couch, leaning on the left elbow.
176. A Roman dinner could last for hours.
177. The Romans even had an expression for a full meal: from the egg to the apples. [F27]
178. Eggs to start. Fruit to finish. And everything in between.
179. And Roman hosts took it personally when you didn't show up.
180. Pliny the Younger once wrote a mock-angry letter to a friend who accepted his dinner invitation and never came. [F35]
181. He listed everything that went to waste: a lettuce each, three snails, two eggs, olives, beets, onions. [F35]
182. And entertainment: a reader, an actor, or a lyre player. [F35]
183. Instead, the friend went to another house for oysters, sea urchins and Spanish dancers. [F35]
184. Honestly, it's hard to blame him.
185. And in one Roman novel, a ridiculously rich man called Trimalchio has a clock in his dining room. [F36]
186. And a trumpeter, to announce how much of his life he has just lost. [F36]
187. Even in fiction, the Romans were a little obsessed with time.
188. But most Romans were not rich.
189. Many apartments had no real kitchen. Fire on the third floor of a building held up by props was a terrible idea.
190. So ordinary people ate simply: porridge, bread, beans, vegetables, and cheap wine mixed with water.
191. Or a hot meal from the street bar on the corner.
192. And the tenth hour? Martial has a special plan for it.
193. "The tenth hour," he writes, "is the hour for my little books." [F2]
194. He's hoping the emperor will read his poems at dinner, relaxed, with a cup of wine in his hand. [F2]
195. A whole poem about the Roman day, and it ends with a sales pitch.
196. Some things never change.
## 10b. Dias de folga: pão e circo

197. But this was an ordinary working day. And Rome had a lot of days that were not ordinary.
198. Dozens of days a year were public festivals, many with free shows paid for by the state or by rich politicians.
199. Plays at the theater. Gladiators at the amphitheater.
200. And the biggest show of all: the chariot races at the Circus Maximus.
201. Ancient writers say it held somewhere between a hundred and fifty and two hundred and fifty thousand spectators. [F37]
202. The fans were divided into teams, by color: the Reds, the Whites, the Greens and the Blues.
203. Not everyone was a fan. Pliny the Younger spent race days at home, writing, and was proud of it. [F38]
204. He couldn't understand why thousands of grown men wanted to watch horses run, again and again. [F38]
205. They don't even care about the horses, he wrote. They cheer for a piece of cloth. [F38]
206. If the drivers swapped colors in the middle of the race, the fans would swap with them. [F38]
207. Sound familiar?
208. Juvenal had a darker view. [F39]
209. The Roman people, he wrote, used to hand out armies and offices. [F39]
210. Now they only long for two things: bread, and circuses. [F39]

## 11. A noite

211. And then, the sun goes down.
212. Rome has no street lights.
213. The streets are pitch dark. Rich men walk home with slaves carrying torches. [F28]
214. Juvenal says you'd be foolish to go out to dinner without first writing your will. [F28]
215. Because from any open window above you, someone might throw out a broken pot. [F28]
216. Or empty something much worse.
217. At night, the streets belong to the wagons, the drunks, and the fires.
218. Augustus created a corps of watchmen, the vigiles, to patrol the dark streets and fight fires. [F29]
219. Up in his small room, our Roman lights a clay oil lamp, for a little while.
220. Lamp oil costs money, so the evening is short.
221. He goes to sleep. Down in the street, the wagons start rolling again.
222. And in a few hours, before the roosters, the schoolmaster will start shouting.

## 12. Fechamento (volta ao presente)

223. So, what did ancient Romans do all day?
224. They worked, mostly in the morning.
225. They networked, every single morning.
226. They ate, gossiped, wrote on walls, and went to the baths, almost every afternoon.
227. And a surprising part of their day is still with us.
228. The English word noon comes from the Latin nona hora: the ninth hour. [F18]
229. It once meant mid-afternoon, and over the centuries it slid back to midday. [F18]
230. Siesta comes from the sixth hour.
231. And that hungry man in the Roman comedy, cursing the inventor of the sundial?
232. He sounds a lot like us, staring at a calendar full of meetings.
233. All the sources for this video, from Martial to Seneca to the walls of Pompeii, are in the description.
234. And tell me in the comments: which Roman hour would you want to live in?

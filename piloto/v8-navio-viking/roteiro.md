# What Would YOUR Life Be Like on a Viking Ship?

Roteiro do vídeo 8, **aguardando aprovação** (~14-15 min no ritmo do vídeo 2). Cada linha numerada vira uma cena (uma frase, uma imagem).
Espinha: você é um fazendeiro na Islândia em 985 e aceita a oferta de Erik, o Vermelho, de colonizar a "Terra Verde". A viagem em si (navio de carga e não o drakkar do cinema, navegação sem bússola, comida, sono, enjoo, 11 navios que não chegam), a vida na Groenlândia e o marfim de morsa, os vikings como mercadores (Ibn Fadlan, dirhams), Vinlândia e L'Anse aux Meadows, e os mitos (chifres, crânios, Eddas). Tese própria no fecho: o que importava não era a espada, era o navio, e os vikings eram uma rede e não um país.
Os marcadores [F1], [F2]... apontam para a tabela de fontes no fim. **Checagem feita em 2026-10-12**, detalhes em [../../pesquisa/checagem-v8-navio-viking.md](../../pesquisa/checagem-v8-navio-viking.md).

Cuidados de imagem: nada de combate, sangue ou corpos. O elmo é sempre uma calota redonda de ferro com protetor de nariz. O navio da viagem é o knarr (casco largo, uma vela quadrada); o navio longo e estreito só aparece nas cenas de navio de guerra. Mapas e números em cartão de poucas palavras.

## 0. Abertura (gancho: uma oferta de imóvel)

1. It's the summer of the year nine hundred eighty-five.
2. You are a farmer in western Iceland, and things are not going great.
3. Then a neighbor knocks on your door.
4. He's known as Erik the Red. He has big plans, and a few killings in his past. [F1]
5. And he has a real estate offer.
6. Free land. Huge farms. Plenty of grass for your cows. A fresh start.
7. The place is called Greenland. Of course you say yes.
8. Now you only have to get there, across the open ocean, in a wooden boat, with your family and your animals.
9. Today, we'll sail with you.
10. But I have to warn you: the ship you're picturing is not the ship you'll get.
11. And by the end, you'll see why most of what you know about Vikings is slightly off.

## 1. O vendedor de terras

12. Erik Thorvaldsson, called Erik the Red, was born in Norway. [F1]
13. His father had to leave after some killings, so the family moved to Iceland. [F1]
14. Erik did not learn the lesson.
15. After a feud that ended in killings, an assembly banished him from Iceland for three years. [F1]
16. Erik sailed west, to see what was out there.
17. For three winters, he explored the coast of Greenland, and found long fjords and no neighbors. [F1]
18. When his exile ended, he went back to Iceland to find customers.
19. Now, how do you sell a huge island that is mostly ice?
20. Simple. You pick a better name.
21. According to the saga, he called it Greenland. [F2]
22. His reasoning, in the saga's words: people will want to go there if the land has a good name. [F2]
23. It's a marketing trick that still works today.
24. And it worked. In the year nine hundred eighty-five or six, a fleet of twenty-five ships sets out, carrying families. [F3]
25. Only fourteen make it. We'll get to the other eleven. [F3]

## 2. O navio errado

26. When you hear "Viking ship," you probably see a long, sleek warship with a carved animal head on the front.
27. That's the longship. It's fast, narrow, and built for warriors.
28. It's also not what you're boarding.
29. Written sources say only one type of ship sailed to Greenland: the knarr. [F6]
30. The knarr is a cargo ship. It's wider than a warship, and built to carry loads. [F6]
31. The best preserved one we have is called Skuldelev one, built in Norway around the year one thousand thirty. [F6]
32. The Viking Ship Museum in Denmark built a replica, named Ottar. [F6]
33. It's about fifty feet long and sixteen feet wide, and a crew of six to fifteen can sail it. [F6]
34. It has one big square sail. And four oars. Four. [F6]
35. The oars are for docking, and for emergencies.
36. Now put thirty or forty people on it. The sagas describe whole families moving together. [F4]
37. Add the animals. On one saga voyage, the settlers took "cattle of all kinds." [F5]
38. There are no cabins. No walls. No roof.
39. It's a camping trip, with cows.
40. The hull is made of planks that overlap and are nailed together. [F9]
41. It's a clever design, which is lucky, because you're about to trust it with your life.

## 3. O barco de guerra (e a velocidade)

42. Of course, longships were real too.
43. In Denmark, the museum rebuilt one. It's called the Sea Stallion from Glendalough. [F7]
44. It copies a warship whose wreck was found in a Danish fjord. The oak came from near Dublin. [F7]
45. It's thirty meters long, almost a hundred feet, with sixty oars, and less than four meters wide. [F7]
46. In two thousand seven, a crew of sixty-five sailed it from Denmark to Dublin, in seven weeks. [F7]
47. When the wind died, a support boat towed it more than five hundred kilometers. [F14]
48. Even the replica needed a tow.
49. The Gokstad ship, found in Norway in eighteen eighty, is smaller: about eighty feet, with thirty-two oars. [F8]
50. In eighteen ninety-three, Norwegians built a copy and sailed it across the Atlantic to Chicago. [F8]
51. The captain recorded speeds of ten to eleven knots. [F8]
52. That's about twelve miles an hour, without a single engine.
53. The crossing took about four weeks. [F8]
54. So longships were for speed, for rivers, and for surprise.
55. Knarrs were for moving house.

## 4. Sem bússola

56. Okay. You're on board. Now, how do you find Greenland?
57. As far as we know, you have no compass. [F11] No map. No GPS.
58. What you have is directions, passed from sailor to sailor.
59. A medieval Icelandic book, the Landnámabók, includes sailing instructions. [F10]
60. For a ship from Norway, it says: head due west. [F10]
61. Along the way, you should see the Shetland Islands in clear weather. [F10]
62. You should be so far from the Faroes that you see only half of the hills. [F10]
63. And you should be close enough to Iceland to see whales and birds. [F10]
64. Your GPS is a whale. Or a bird.
65. From Iceland, the book says the crossing is shortest from a peninsula in the west, and takes four days, if everything goes right. [F10]
66. Historians think sailors tried to hold one latitude, and follow it west. [F10]
67. But what if it's cloudy?
68. Some sagas mention a "sunstone." [F11]
69. In twenty eleven, researchers showed a crystal called Iceland spar can find the sun's direction, even through clouds. [F11]
70. But nobody has ever found a sunstone on a Viking ship. [F11]
71. So maybe. We just don't know.
72. Fog is worse. A man named Bjarni Herjolfsson was sailing to Greenland when fog and wind pushed his ship off course for days. [F12]
73. When land finally appeared, it wasn't Greenland. [F12]
74. In the sagas, that wrong turn is how Europeans first see North America. [F12]
75. Moral: Viking navigation worked. Until it didn't.

## 5. Comida, sono e enjoo

76. The Roskilde museum says sea food was porridge, flour, dried fish, butter, and bread. [F13]
77. Boiled or roasted meat was rare. [F13]
78. To drink? Water, kept in barrels. [F13]
79. Beer? Only on the ships of chieftains and kings. [F13]
80. Modern crews cook on gas rings. The museum says that's "in contrast to the Viking cooks." [F13]
81. So: porridge, dry fish, and water. For weeks.
82. Now, where do you sleep?
83. Right there, on the open deck, probably wrapped in a wool cloak. [F16]
84. When the Sea Stallion crew sailed to Dublin, they slept in an open boat with almost no room. [F14]
85. Their food was preserved rations, and some days brought what the project manager called "cold, lashing rain." [F14]
86. One sailor said that after eighteen hours, he was so cold and wet he wanted to quit. [F14]
87. Now imagine that, with children.
88. Then there's seasickness. On a trial trip to Norway, the Sea Stallion's nurse recorded almost thirty cases. [F15]
89. Eight in ten crew members felt nauseous, mostly in moderate winds. [F15]
90. And those were volunteers who had signed up for this.

## 6. Quatorze de vinte e cinco

91. Now, the part nobody puts in the brochure.
92. Of the twenty-five ships that left Iceland, only fourteen reached Greenland. [F3]
93. The sources don't say exactly what happened to the rest.
94. Some may have turned back and tried again later, and others may have been lost at sea. [F3]
95. There's no coast guard. There's no radio.
96. If your ship sinks, the only rescue is another ship, and that ship has its own problems.
97. But you're lucky. You are on one of the fourteen.
98. After days at sea, you see ice, then mountains, then green slopes.
99. You made it. Welcome to Greenland.

## 7. Groenlândia

100. Erik's own farm is called Brattahlid, which means "steep slope." It's the center of the colony. [F18]
101. At its peak, the main settlement had about five hundred farms. [F18]
102. A second one, farther north, had around ninety. [F18]
103. A farm means sheep, goats, and cattle, in a place where winter is long.
104. Life is hard, and it's not only the weather.
105. In Erik the Red's saga, a fever breaks out on a farm in Greenland. [F17]
106. There's no hospital to call.
107. So why would anyone stay?
108. Because Greenland has something Europe wants: walrus ivory. [F19]
109. DNA studies suggest Greenland had a near monopoly on Europe's walrus ivory for over two hundred years. [F19]
110. In the eleven twenties, Greenlanders reportedly used ivory to get their own bishop from the king of Norway. [F19]
111. So the settlers weren't hermits at the end of the world.
112. They were suppliers.

## 8. Mercadores, não só saqueadores

113. And that brings us to the biggest myth of all.
114. When you hear "Vikings," you think raiders.
115. And yes, raids happened. In seven hundred ninety-three, raiders hit the monastery on Lindisfarne, in England. [F23]
116. Historians often use that date to mark the start of the Viking Age. [F23]
117. But in Old Norse, "viking" was something you did. It meant going on an expedition overseas, often a raid. Not a nationality. Not a job title. [F24]
118. Most people in Scandinavia weren't vikings at all. They were farmers, fishers, and craftspeople. [F24]
119. Around the year nine hundred twenty-two, an Arab envoy named Ibn Fadlan met a group of traders called the Rus, on the Volga River. [F21]
120. He wrote that their bodies were perfect, "like palm trees." [F21]
121. He was less impressed by their hygiene.
122. Every morning, a slave girl brought a basin of water to a man, then carried it to the next one. [F21]
123. Each man washed his face and hair in it, blew his nose, and spat. Then it moved on. [F21]
124. Ibn Fadlan called it the filthiest water imaginable. He may have been exaggerating. [F21]
125. He also saw what they sold: sable furs, and enslaved people. [F21]
126. That part of the story is not romantic.
127. In return, they got silver coins from the Arab world. [F22]
128. On the Swedish island of Gotland, archaeologists have found hundreds of silver hoards, many full of Arab coins. [F22]
129. A coin from the Middle East, buried on a Baltic island. That's a trade network.

## 9. Vinlândia

130. Back to Greenland, and to Erik's family.
131. According to the sagas, Erik's son Leif goes to Norway, around the year one thousand. [F25]
132. The Norwegian king sends him home to bring Christianity to Greenland. [F25]
133. About the same time, Iceland's assembly decides the whole island will be Christian. [F25]
134. Then Leif wants to find the lands Bjarni saw in the fog. [F25]
135. On the way to the ship, Erik's horse stumbles, and he falls. [F25]
136. He says he's not meant to find more lands than the one they already live in. [F25]
137. He goes home. The man who found Greenland never sees what's beyond it.
138. Leif sails with about thirty-five men. [F25]
139. The sagas say they find wild wheat and grapevines, and call the land Vinland. [F26]
140. Later, another expedition follows, led by Karlsefni: sixty men and five women. [F26]
141. They trade with the people already living there. The locals want red cloth, and give furs. [F26]
142. But the saga says it doesn't last. After three winters, the settlers leave. [F26]
143. In the saga's words, there would always be "war and terror" hanging over them. [F26]
144. For a long time, nobody knew if any of this was real.
145. Then, in nineteen sixty, a Norwegian explorer, Helge Ingstad, and his wife, the archaeologist Anne Stine Ingstad, found a site called L'Anse aux Meadows, in Newfoundland, Canada. [F27]
146. Eight buildings made of sod over a wooden frame. A forge. A carpentry shop. And a spot for repairing boats. [F27]
147. In twenty twenty-one, scientists used a solar storm recorded in tree rings to date cut wood from the site. [F28]
148. The result: the trees were cut in the year one thousand twenty-one, with metal tools. [F28]
149. So far, that's the earliest year we can prove Europeans were in the Americas. [F28]
150. And in the ruins, they found butternuts. Those trees don't grow in Newfoundland. [F29]
151. The nearest ones grow in New Brunswick, so someone went even farther south. [F29]

## 10. Mitos

152. So let's clear up a few myths.
153. Myth one: horned helmets.
154. The horns come from a costume designer, Carl Emil Doepler, who added them to Viking characters in Wagner's opera in eighteen seventy-six. [F30]
155. The best preserved Viking helmet we have, from a grave in Gjermundbu, Norway, is a plain round iron cap. No horns. [F30]
156. Scandinavia did have horned helmets, but from the Bronze Age, over a thousand years earlier. [F30]
157. Myth two: drinking from skulls.
158. A seventeenth century scholar misread a line of Old Norse poetry that meant drinking horns, and thought it meant skulls. [F31]
159. Myth three: we know exactly what Vikings believed.
160. Most of what we know about Norse gods comes from the Eddas. [F32]
161. They were written down in the thirteenth century, by Christian Icelanders. [F32]
162. That's more than two hundred years after your trip.
163. So when someone tells you exactly what a Viking believed, ask for the source.

## 11. Fechamento (a tese)

164. Let's go back to your boat.
165. You crossed an ocean on a cargo ship, with your family, your cows, and a lot of porridge.
166. You didn't cross to conquer anyone. You crossed to farm.
167. That's the real surprise.
168. Vikings are famous for swords. But the technology that mattered was the ship.
169. A ship that could cross open sea, sail up a river, and land on a beach with no harbor. [F6]
170. It connected farms in Greenland to markets in Europe, and furs in Russia to silver in Baghdad. [F19] [F22]
171. The Vikings weren't a country. They were a network, and the ship was the internet.
172. And networks have a weakness.
173. Historians think falling demand for walrus ivory was one reason Greenland faded. [F20]
174. No Greenland tusks are known to have reached Europe after thirteen twenty-seven. [F20]
175. By the end of the fifteenth century, the Norse in Greenland had vanished. [F20]
176. Erik's sales pitch lasted about five hundred years.
177. Not bad for a man with a criminal record and a very good name for a very icy island.
178. If you enjoyed this voyage, tell me in the comments: which journey in history should we take next?

# What Would YOUR Life Be Like in Pompeii on the Last Day?

Vídeo 7 do canal (~15 min no ritmo dos vídeos 2 e 5). Cada linha numerada vira uma cena (uma frase, uma imagem).
Espinha: as últimas 24 horas de Pompeia, hora a hora, vistas por Felix, padeiro liberto fictício, em segunda pessoa ("você"). As cartas de Plínio, o Jovem, a Tácito (Epistulae 6.16 e 6.20) dão o relógio da erupção; as paredes de Pompeia (grafites, avisos eleitorais, termopólios, padarias) dão o dia a dia. A data é tratada com honestidade: tradição de 24 de agosto de 79 contra o grafite a carvão de 2018 (17 de outubro) e o experimento do Parco que enfraqueceu o argumento. Tese do fecho: o último dia de uma cidade não parece o último dia; a história de Pompeia vem de gente comum escrevendo em paredes.
Os marcadores [F1], [F2]... apontam para a tabela de fontes no fim. **Checagem feita em 2026-10-12**, ver [../../pesquisa/checagem-v7-pompeia.md](../../pesquisa/checagem-v7-pompeia.md).

Cuidados de imagem: nada de corpos, moldes de gesso desenhados ou sofrimento explícito (a linha 161 marca o limite: "this is where we stop drawing"). O vulcão e a nuvem aparecem de longe, em desenho simples. As cenas ficam neutras quanto à estação (sem roupa pesada nem fruta de outono), porque a data é disputada. Textos nas paredes entram só como cartões de código, com poucas palavras.

## 0. Abertura (gancho: o último dia da sua cidade)

1. Good morning. Today is the last day of your city.
2. Nobody has told you.
3. You are Felix, a baker in Pompeii, a busy Roman town on the Bay of Naples.
4. Somewhere between ten and twenty thousand people live here, depending on which expert you ask. [F1]
5. You used to be a slave. Now you are free, you earn a small wage, and you have a long to-do list.
6. Buy flour. Complain about prices. Vote for the right candidate. Maybe go see the gladiators.
7. What is not on your list is "run away from the volcano."
8. Because the big green mountain behind your town looks harmless.
9. A Greek geographer who wrote about it generations earlier saw burnt-looking rocks, and guessed that its fire had died out. [F2]
10. So today, we will live your day, hour by hour.
11. Our guide is Pliny the Younger, who wrote two letters about it. They are the only eyewitness account that survives. [F3]
12. And here is the twist: he never once says the word "Pompeii." [F4]
13. So for the town itself, we will have to read its walls.
14. But first, a problem. Which day is today?

## 1. Que dia é hoje?

15. The traditional answer is the twenty-fourth of August, in the year seventy-nine. [F5]
16. That date comes from Pliny's letter to the historian Tacitus. [F5]
17. But Pliny's original letter is lost. We only have copies, written out by hand centuries later. [F5]
18. The oldest copies agree on August. Some later ones say other months. [F5]
19. Then, in 2018, archaeologists found a message on a wall.
20. It was written in charcoal, inside a house that was being renovated. [F6]
21. It gave a date: the seventeenth of October. [F6]
22. Charcoal rubs off easily. So the team thought it must have been written just before the eruption. [F6]
23. Add some autumn fruit found in the ruins, like pomegranates, and October looked likely. [F7]
24. Then the park decided to test the idea.
25. They wrote their own charcoal note on that same wall, and checked it month after month. [F8]
26. Ten months later, it was still perfectly readable. [F8]
27. So the old message could have been written the October before, not the October after. [F8]
28. And the fruit could have been late-season, or stored. [F7]
29. Today, the park's director says August is still the best-supported date. But he admits that Pliny could be wrong. [F9]
30. So the honest answer is: probably August, but not certain.
31. We will use August, because Pliny gives us the hours.
32. Felix does not care about any of this. He has bread to bake.

## 2. Antes do amanhecer: a padaria

33. Your day starts in the dark, at the bakery.
34. Pompeii has at least thirty-one of them. [F10]
35. Yours is small: an oven, a counter, and a back room with a grain mill.
36. The mill is a big stone shaped like an hourglass. Somebody has to keep it turning.
37. Usually that is a donkey. Sometimes it is a person. [F11]
38. In 2023, archaeologists announced a bakery of the darkest kind. [F11]
39. Enslaved people and donkeys worked together in a room with no outside door, and with small, high, barred windows. [F11]
40. You know a world like that. You used to live in one.
41. Today, at least, you decide when your bread comes out of the oven.
42. The loaves are round, and cut with lines into wedges, so people can break off a piece. [F12]
43. In one Pompeian bakery, eighty-one loaves were found still in the oven, turned to charcoal. [F12]
44. Some bakers pressed a stamp into the dough, like a signature. [F13]
45. In nearby Herculaneum, one loaf says: "Celer, slave of Quintus Granius Verus." [F13]
46. So even bread had an author. And some of those authors were slaves.
47. Now, about pizza.
48. In 2023, archaeologists found a painting of a flatbread on a table, next to wine and fruit. [F14]
49. The headlines said "pizza." [F14]
50. The park's director said: not pizza. Maybe a distant ancestor. [F14]
51. No tomatoes. No mozzarella. Sorry.

## 3. Manhã: as paredes falam

52. At sunrise, you open the shutters and step outside.
53. The street is crowded with carts, donkeys, and people.
54. And after rain, it is a river of dirty water and animal waste. [F15]
55. That is why the street has big stepping stones set across it. [F15]
56. You hop from stone to stone, and the cart wheels roll between them. [F15]
57. For water, you walk to the fountain on the corner. There are more than twenty-five in town. [F16]
58. Their water arrives by aqueduct, from far away, since around twenty BC. [F16]
59. Then you look up. The walls are talking.
60. Painted in red, there are election notices. More than two thousand six hundred survive. [F17]
61. Most back candidates for aedile, who looks after streets and markets, or for duumvir, a top local magistrate. [F17]
62. Groups endorse them. The worshippers of Isis back a man named Helvius Sabinus. [F18]
63. Who gets to vote? Free men who are citizens. Not women. Not slaves. [F19]
64. Then the walls get personal.
65. Somebody wrote: "I admire you, wall, for not having collapsed under the weight of so many scribblers." [F20]
66. Remember that wall. It will come back.
67. Another notice says: "The late drinkers ask you to elect Marcus Cerrinius Vatia." [F18]
68. Pompeii does not just have walls. It has a comment section.

## 4. Meio da manhã: dinheiro, libertos e um menino de seis anos

69. By mid-morning, you deliver bread to a fancy house.
70. Painted walls, a garden, and a very proud owner.
71. Maybe he was a slave once too. In Pompeii, that could happen.
72. The House of the Vettii, one of the richest homes in town, belonged to two men who had been freed from slavery. [F21]
73. Historians think they made their money from wine. [F21]
74. Freed slaves became citizens. But they could not hold public office. [F19]
75. So what do you do with money, and no political career?
76. You find another way in.
77. About seventeen years ago, a violent earthquake shook the region. [F22]
78. Even now, the town is still partly under repair. [F22]
79. One of the damaged buildings was the temple of Isis.
80. A freedman named Numerius Popidius Ampliatus paid to rebuild it, in his son's name. [F23]
81. In return, the town council admitted the boy as a member, free of charge. [F23]
82. The boy was six years old. [F23]
83. A six-year-old city councillor. That is one way to beat the system.
84. Meanwhile, a banker named Caecilius Iucundus keeps his records on wax tablets. [F24]
85. A hundred and fifty-three of them were found in his house. Auction receipts, and loans. [F24]
86. Pompeii runs on favors, contracts, and debts. Sounds familiar.

## 5. Meio-dia: almoço no bar

87. At noon, you get hungry.
88. Pompeii has about eighty snack bars, called thermopolia. [F25]
89. You lean on a stone counter with big clay jars sunk into it, full of hot food. [F25]
90. In 2020, archaeologists fully dug up one of these shops. [F25]
91. On its counter, someone painted two ducks hanging upside down, and a rooster. [F25]
92. The jars held the bones of pig, goat, fish, and snails. And a little duck. [F25]
93. So the painted menu was real. Truth in advertising, two thousand years ago.
94. The counter also shows a painted dog on a leash. [F25]
95. And scratched on its frame, there is an insult aimed at a man named Nicias. [F26]
96. Pompeii never lacked critics.
97. In one wine jar, they found crushed beans. A Roman cookbook says beans could change the wine's taste and color. [F25]
98. And on almost everything goes garum, the fish sauce.
99. One Pompeian producer, Aulus Umbricius Scaurus, labeled his jars as the best. [F27]
100. Think of ketchup, but made of fish.
101. Then you eat, chat, and gossip. The break is over fast.

## 6. Início da tarde: a arena

102. After lunch, everybody talks about the games.
103. Pompeii loves its gladiators. The walls say so.
104. "The girls' idol, Celadus the Thracian." [F28]
105. Another notice says a troupe will fight on the thirty-first of May. "There will be a hunt, and awnings." [F28]
106. Awnings. Even then, people wanted shade.
107. And fans took all of this very seriously.
108. In the year fifty-nine, fans from Pompeii and the nearby town of Nuceria started insulting each other during a show. [F29]
109. Insults turned into stones. Stones turned into swords. [F29]
110. The Pompeians won.
111. The Roman Senate was not amused. It banned shows in Pompeii for ten years. [F29]
112. Some historians doubt the ban was strict. [F29]
113. And somebody painted the riot on a wall in a house, like a sports highlight. [F29]

## 7. Uma da tarde: a nuvem

114. Around one in the afternoon, something changes. [F30]
115. Across the bay, at Misenum, Pliny's mother points out a strange cloud. [F30]
116. She is talking to her brother, Pliny the Elder, an admiral and a famous scholar. [F30]
117. He walks out to get a better look.
118. His nephew compares the cloud to a pine tree: a tall trunk, with branches spreading at the top. [F30]
119. Sometimes it looked bright, sometimes dark and spotted, with earth and cinders. [F30]
120. Today, this kind of eruption is called Plinian, after the nephew. [F31]
121. Scientists estimate that the column rose roughly thirty kilometers. [F32]
122. From Pompeii, you do not need to look across a bay. It is right there, and the wind is blowing toward you. [F32]
123. What do you do? What would you do?
124. You stare. You ask the neighbors. Somebody says it is nothing.
125. There is no alarm, no news, no emergency app. Just a huge cloud, and many opinions.
126. Soon, small pieces of white pumice begin to fall, like light hail. [F32]
127. Pumice is volcanic rock full of air bubbles. It is light.
128. It looks harmless. For a while.

## 8. A tarde: chuva de pedra

129. The pumice keeps falling for about twelve hours. [F32]
130. The layer grows by roughly twelve to fifteen centimeters every hour. [F32]
131. Those stepping stones in the street? They disappear first.
132. By one in the morning, the pumice around Pompeii is about a meter and a half deep. [F32]
133. Roofs start to give way under the weight. [F33]
134. So you face an awful choice. Stay inside, and risk the roof. Go outside, and risk the falling stones. [F34]
135. Pliny the Elder's group, at Stabiae, chose to go outside. [F34]
136. They tied pillows to their heads with napkins. That was their whole protection. [F34]
137. It was daytime everywhere else. There, it was darker than any night, and they carried torches. [F34]
138. Pliny the Elder had sailed in to rescue a friend named Rectina. [F35]
139. His pilot suggested turning back. He answered: "Fortune favors the brave." [F35]
140. He died on that shore. His nephew says that, when the body was found, he looked like a man asleep. [F35]
141. In Pompeii, everyone is making their own choice.
142. Many pack a few coins and leave, by the roads and by sea.
143. We know some of them survived, because Pompeian names turn up on inscriptions in other towns of the region. [F36]
144. Others stay. Maybe waiting for the storm to pass. Maybe unable to leave.
145. We cannot know what Felix would choose. He is invented. But the evidence suggests most of the town got out. [F37]

## 9. A noite: a escuridão

146. Night falls, and the darkness gets worse than night.
147. Pliny the Younger, eighteen years old, is at Misenum, across the bay. [F3]
148. He later wrote that it was not like a night with clouds, or no moon. It was like a closed room with all the lights out. [F38]
149. You could hear women wailing, children screaming, and men shouting. [F38]
150. Some begged the gods for help. Many were sure that there were no gods left, and that this was the eternal night. [F38]
151. And Pliny? He was reading Livy, and taking notes. [F38]
152. A friend scolded him for being so calm. He kept reading. [F38]
153. Overachiever. Or in shock. Probably both.
154. Meanwhile, the ground keeps shaking.
155. In his letter, he says there had been tremors for days, but nobody was alarmed, because they were normal in Campania. [F39]
156. That is the thing about warnings. You only recognize them afterward.
157. About one in the morning, a surge of hot ash and gas rushes down the volcano and overwhelms Herculaneum, on the coast. [F40]
158. Pompeii is farther away. For a few more hours, the mountain gives you time.

## 10. A manhã seguinte

159. By half past six, there is more than two meters of pumice in Pompeii. [F32]
160. Around seven thirty, the fourth surge reaches the city. [F40]
161. This is where we stop drawing.
162. Felix is invented. The people of Pompeii were not.
163. About thirteen hundred victims have been found there so far. [F37]
164. The park estimates that roughly one in ten of the city's people died, and more may have died while fleeing. [F37]
165. That means most of the town probably escaped. And lost everything.
166. In 1863, the archaeologist Giuseppe Fiorelli began making plaster casts of the hollows left in the hardened ash. [F41]
167. Modern scans show the casts were reworked. They are records, not photographs. [F41]
168. They are not statues. They are the last trace of real people, and they deserve respect.
169. And one more thing: Pompeii was not buried by lava. It was pumice, then hot ash. [F42]

## 11. Depois: o que Pompeia nos ensina

170. For centuries, the town stayed buried.
171. Around 1592, an architect digging a water channel hit its walls, and said nothing. [F43]
172. Proper digging began in 1748. It took until 1763 for an inscription to name the city. [F43]
173. But Pompeii is not a perfect snapshot of one afternoon.
174. The classicist Mary Beard says it was disturbed, evacuated, and pillaged. [F44]
175. In 2025, the park announced that people came back to live in the ruins, in the upper floors, for a long time. [F45]
176. So the story of Pompeii does not end with the ash.
177. What strikes me is how normal it all was.
178. Bakers, bar owners, voters, gladiator fans, borrowers, and a six-year-old councillor.
179. And one person who wrote on a wall to complain about all the other people writing on walls.
180. That grumpy writer was wrong. The scribbles survived. We can still read them. [F20]
181. The last day of a city does not look like the last day. It looks like an ordinary morning, with a long to-do list.
182. Felix never knew he was in the last chapter. Neither do we.
183. If you enjoyed this day, tell me in the comments: which day in history should we live next?

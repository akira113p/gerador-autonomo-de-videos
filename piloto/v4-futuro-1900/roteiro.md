# How People in 1900 Imagined the Year 2000

Proposta de roteiro (~15–16 min no ritmo do vídeo 2), **aguardando aprovação**. Cada linha numerada vira uma cena (uma frase, uma imagem).
Espinha: o artigo "What May Happen in the Next Hundred Years", de John Elfreth Watkins Jr. (Ladies' Home Journal, dezembro de 1900), com um placar de acertos e erros. Em volta: os cartões franceses "En l'an 2000" (1899–1910), a ficção da época e os visionários.
Os marcadores [F1], [F2]... apontam para a tabela de fontes no fim. **Checagem feita em 2026-10-11**, ver [../../pesquisa/checagem-3-roteiros-propostos.md](../../pesquisa/checagem-3-roteiros-propostos.md). As previsões do Watkins foram conferidas contra a transcrição do artigo (earlyradiohistory.us); falta conferir no fac-símile da página.

Vantagem visual: os cartões "En l'an 2000" e as páginas da revista são de domínio público e podem entrar como acervo real, ao lado das imagens geradas.

## 0. Abertura (gancho: a revista)

1. December, 1900. A new century is about to begin.
2. In homes across America, people open the December issue of a popular magazine: the Ladies' Home Journal. [F1]
3. Inside, there's an article with a bold title: "What May Happen in the Next Hundred Years." [F1]
4. The writer, John Elfreth Watkins Junior, lays out what he thinks the world will look like in the year 2000. [F1]
5. Some of their predictions are so accurate, they're a little creepy.
6. Others are completely, gloriously wrong.
7. Strawberries as big as apples. An alphabet without the letter C.
8. So today, we're going to do something fun.
9. We're going to read the future from the past, and keep score.
10. Right. Wrong. Or weirdly close.
11. And we'll meet French artists who drew flying firemen, and a novelist who put the words "credit card" in print in 1888.
12. But first, let's see the world these people were living in.

## 1. O mundo de 1900

13. In 1900, the average American could expect to live to about forty-seven. [F2]
14. Most people lived on farms or in small towns.
15. Most homes had no electricity, and no telephone. [F3]
16. Light came from oil lamps or gas. Heat came from coal or wood.
17. The streets of big cities were full of horses.
18. And full of what horses leave behind.
19. Cars existed, but they were rare: only a few thousand in the whole country. [F4]
20. The Wright brothers hadn't flown yet. That would only happen three years later. [F5]
21. But people in 1900 had just lived through an incredible century.
22. Trains, the telegraph, photography, the telephone, the electric light bulb.
23. In a single lifetime, the world had changed more than in the thousand years before.
24. That same year, Paris held a huge World's Fair. [F6]
25. Visitors rode a moving sidewalk, an electric walkway that carried them around the fairground. [F6]
26. Some saw early films with recorded sound. [F6]
27. Tens of millions of people visited. [F6]
28. So the mood was simple: if the last hundred years were amazing, the next hundred will be even better.
29. That's the mood in which Watkins wrote his article.
30. Let's open it.

## 2. Watkins: os acertos

31. Prediction number one. Photographs will be telegraphed from any distance. [F1]
32. Watkins wrote that if there's a battle in China a hundred years later, photos of it would appear in the newspapers an hour later. [F1]
33. In 1900, sending a photo across the ocean meant putting it on a ship, and waiting weeks.
34. By the year 2000, news photos traveled around the world in seconds.
35. Watkins was actually too modest. Not an hour later. Almost instantly.
36. Score: right.
37. Prediction number two. Man will see around the world. [F1]
38. Watkins described cameras connected electrically to screens, thousands of miles away. [F1]
39. That's basically television. And, if you squint, a video call.
40. Score: right.
41. Prediction number three. Wireless telephones will span the world. [F1]
42. He imagined a husband in the middle of the Atlantic Ocean, talking to his wife sitting in her boudoir in Chicago. [F1]
43. In 1900, radio was brand new. Marconi had only been sending simple signals in Morse code for a few years. [F7]
44. A wireless voice call from the middle of the ocean was pure fantasy.
45. By 2000, millions of people carried a wireless phone in their pocket.
46. Score: right.
47. Ready-cooked meals will be bought from establishments similar to bakeries. [F1]
48. Delivered to private houses, hot or cold, by pneumatic tube or automobile wagon. [F1]
49. Fast food. Frozen dinners. Delivery apps.
50. Score: right. Very right.
51. Prediction number five. Hot and cold air from spigots. [F1]
52. Watkins wrote that hot or cold air would be turned on from spigots to regulate the temperature of a house. [F1]
53. Modern air conditioning was invented only two years later, in 1902, by Willis Carrier, for a printing plant in Brooklyn. [F8]
54. By 2000, most American homes had it.
55. Score: right.
56. Prediction number six. Automobiles will be cheaper than horses. [F1]
57. In 1900, a car was a toy for the rich.
58. Then, in 1908, Henry Ford launched the Model T, and kept making it cheaper. [F9]
59. Within a couple of decades, the horse was disappearing from city streets.
60. Score: right.
61. So far, Watkins is doing amazingly well.
62. And now, the fun part.

## 3. Watkins: os erros

63. Prediction number seven. There will be no C, X or Q in our everyday alphabet. [F1]
64. They'll be abandoned, he said, because they're unnecessary. [F1]
65. This came from the spelling reform movement, which was very popular at the time. [F10]
66. Its supporters wanted to spell words exactly the way they sound.
67. Even President Theodore Roosevelt tried it, a few years later. He ordered government printers to use simplified spellings. [F10]
68. Congress pushed back, and he gave up. [F10]
69. The C, the X and the Q are still here. And so is "through," with all its silent letters.
70. Score: very wrong.
71. Prediction number eight. Strawberries as large as apples. [F1]
72. And peas as large as beets. [F1]
73. Fruit did get bigger, and modern strawberries are much bigger than wild ones.
74. But as big as apples? Not quite. Not yet.
75. Score: wrong. But with a little sympathy.
76. Prediction number nine. There will be no mosquitoes and no flies. [F1]
77. Watkins said insect screens would be unnecessary, because house-flies, mosquitoes and roaches would be practically exterminated. [F1]
78. Rats and mice, too, he said, would be exterminated. [F1]
79. Anyone who has lived in a big city knows how that went.
80. Score: wrong. Painfully wrong.
81. Prediction number ten. Everyone will be able to walk ten miles. [F1]
82. Watkins said that a man or woman who couldn't walk ten miles at a stretch would be considered a weakling. [F1]
83. Ten miles is about sixteen kilometers.
84. Today, many people drive to the gym, so they can walk on a machine.
85. Score: wrong.
86. Prediction number eleven. There will be no wild animals, except in zoos. [F1]
87. That one went wrong in a good way. There are still wild animals.
88. But it shows something important: in 1900, many people thought wild nature was simply going to disappear.
89. Score: thankfully wrong.

## 4. Watkins: estranhamente perto

90. Then there are the predictions that are neither right nor wrong.
91. Just weirdly close.
92. Watkins said Americans would grow taller, by one or two inches. [F1]
93. And in fact, Americans did get taller during the twentieth century, by roughly that much. [F11]
94. Score: weirdly close.
95. He said there would be between three hundred and fifty and five hundred million people in "America and its possessions." [F1]
96. He was counting on Mexico, Nicaragua and other countries joining the Union. That never happened. The United States itself had about two hundred and eighty million in 2000. [F12]
97. So he was off. But he was right that the population would explode.
98. And he said people would live longer: fifty years, instead of thirty-five. [F1]
99. Here, he was too pessimistic.
100. By 2000, life expectancy in the United States was over seventy-five. [F2]
101. Antibiotics, vaccines, clean water. Things that even the experts of 1900 couldn't imagine.
102. Score: wrong, but in the best possible direction.
103. Final score for Watkins: a lot better than you'd expect.
104. But he wasn't the only one dreaming about the year 2000.
105. And the others were much stranger.

## 5. Os cartões franceses: En l'an 2000

106. Around the time of the Paris World's Fair, a toy maker in Lyon, Armand Gervais, commissioned a series of illustrated cards called "En l'an 2000." In the year 2000. [F13]
107. Jean-Marc Côté and other French artists drew them. They were meant to be slipped into cigarette boxes, and later sent as postcards. [F13]
108. But they were never distributed. The toy maker died during production, and the cards sat in his closed factory for decades. [F13]
109. In the 1980s, a Canadian writer found a set in a Paris shop and lent it to the science fiction writer Isaac Asimov, who published the cards in a book in 1986. [F13]
110. And they are a wonderful window into the imagination of 1900.
111. In one card, firefighters fly to a burning building on wings. [F13]
112. In another, policemen patrol the sky, also on wings. [F13]
113. There's an air cab, which the caption calls the hansom cab of the future. [F13]
114. And then there's school.
115. In the year 2000, according to one card, the teacher feeds books into a machine. [F13]
116. A boy turns a crank, and the knowledge travels through wires, into headsets on the students' heads. [F13]
117. No reading. No studying. Just download.
118. Honestly, kids today are still waiting for that one.
119. Other cards show a partly automated tailor's shop. [F13]
120. Gatling guns mounted on automobiles. Battleships that look like blimps. [F13]
121. And a few that are just glorious nonsense.
122. Divers trawling the surface of the sea for seagulls. And underwater croquet. [F13]
123. A bus pulled by a whale. [F13]
124. Here's what's interesting about these cards.
125. Look at the people in them.
126. The clothes are still the clothes of 1900.
127. The machines change. Society doesn't.
128. Keep that in mind. We'll come back to it.

## 6. A ficção: o cartão de crédito e o streaming

129. Writers were imagining the future too.
130. In 1888, an American writer named Edward Bellamy published a novel called Looking Backward. [F14]
131. Its hero falls asleep in Boston in 1887, and wakes up in the year 2000. [F14]
132. In nineteenth-century America, only Uncle Tom's Cabin sold more copies in its first years. [F14]
133. Bellamy's year 2000 is a perfect society, which, let's be honest, it wasn't.
134. But two details are amazing.
135. In his future, people don't use cash. They pay with a card, and the book calls it a "credit card." [F14]
136. Not exactly like ours: it works more like a debit card. But in 1888, the idea and the name were already there. [F14]
137. And in his year 2000, people listen to music at home, live, at any hour, through a telephone line. [F14]
138. Concert halls are wired to the houses of the city, with a program that runs around the clock. [F14]
139. That's basically streaming.
140. In 1909, the English writer E. M. Forster went even further, in a short story called The Machine Stops. [F15]
141. In his future, people live alone in small rooms underground.
142. They almost never meet in person. [F15]
143. They talk to each other through screens, give lectures to people they can't touch, and depend completely on a giant machine. [F15]
144. Then, one day, the machine starts to fail.
145. Forster wrote that story more than a hundred years ago.
146. And yet it sounds a lot like the first months of 2020.
147. Even Rudyard Kipling, the author of The Jungle Book, wrote a story set in the year 2000. [F16]
148. In his version, mail is carried across the Atlantic by giant airships. [F16]
149. A lovely idea. And completely wrong.
150. Airships were the future in 1905. Until they weren't.

## 7. Os visionários: Wells, Tesla e Edison

151. Some predictions from this era are so precise, they're almost uncomfortable.
152. H. G. Wells, the author of The War of the Worlds, wrote a short story in 1903 about armored fighting machines crossing trenches. [F17]
153. He called them "land ironclads." [F17]
154. A little more than a decade later, in the First World War, the first tanks rolled across the battlefield. [F17]
155. In 1914, Wells published a novel called The World Set Free. [F18]
156. It describes a new kind of weapon, made from the energy inside atoms. [F18]
157. He called it the "atomic bomb." [F18]
158. And here's the eerie part.
159. A Hungarian physicist named Leo Szilard read that book in 1932. [F18]
160. A year later, walking through London, he had the idea of the nuclear chain reaction, the idea that would make real atomic bombs possible. [F18]
161. He was careful about crediting the book. But he later wrote that he knew what a chain reaction would mean, because he had read H. G. Wells. [F18]
162. Sometimes, science fiction gives people a picture of the future before the science does.
163. Then there's Nikola Tesla.
164. In 1926, he gave an interview to an American magazine. [F19]
165. He said that when wireless was perfected, the whole Earth would become like one huge brain. [F19]
166. And he said a man would be able to carry a device for this in his vest pocket. [F19]
167. In 1926. A pocket device, connecting the planet.
168. Score: frighteningly right.
169. And Thomas Edison, the most famous inventor alive?
170. In an interview in 1911, he predicted that books would be printed on ultra-thin sheets of nickel. [F20]
171. So thin that a single book, two inches thick, could hold forty thousand pages. [F20]
172. He was right about the idea: a whole library you can carry.
173. He was just wrong about the material. It wasn't nickel. It was silicon.

## 8. Por que eles erraram

174. So why did these smart people get so much right, and so much hilariously wrong?
175. There's a pattern.
176. They took the exciting technology of their own time, and made it bigger.
177. In 1900, the exciting things were electricity, wings, airships, and pneumatic tubes.
178. So the future was full of electric everything, people with wings, airships and tubes.
179. What almost nobody saw was the computer.
180. Not the giant machine. The idea of information itself: of words, music, images and money all turning into numbers.
181. That idea changed everything in the twentieth century, and it was almost invisible in 1900.
182. The other mistake was even bigger.
183. They changed the machines, but not the people.
184. Remember the cards: the flying firemen still wear 1900 helmets. The ladies still wear corsets. The maid is still a maid.
185. Very few of these visions imagined the biggest changes of the next hundred years.
186. Women voting and working in every profession. The end of the great empires. Two world wars.
187. People in 1900 imagined a future with better machines.
188. They didn't imagine a future with different people.

## 9. Fechamento: e nós?

189. And before we laugh too hard, let's turn the question around.
190. What will people in the year 2100 think of our predictions?
191. Flying cars. Robot butlers. Cities on Mars.
192. Maybe some of those will happen.
193. And maybe a kid, in 2100, will look at our videos about the future, and laugh at how we imagined it.
194. Not because our machines were wrong.
195. But because, just like in 1900, we imagined the future full of people exactly like us.
196. The future, it turns out, always shows up wearing the clothes of the present.
197. So tell me in the comments: what's one prediction you think we'll get completely wrong?
198. And if you liked traveling back to the future, stay with us. There's a lot more past to explore.

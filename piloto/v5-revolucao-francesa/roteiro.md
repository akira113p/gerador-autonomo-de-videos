# What Was Daily Life Like During the French Revolution?

Proposta de roteiro (~15–16 min no ritmo do vídeo 2), **aguardando aprovação**. Cada linha numerada vira uma cena (uma frase, uma imagem).
Espinha: um dia comum em Paris, 19 Floréal do Ano II (8 de maio de 1794), no auge do Terror, visto por um artesão do Faubourg Saint-Antoine. O dia termina com a execução de Lavoisier, que ajudou a criar o sistema métrico. Mesmo formato do vídeo 2 (Roma): o dia, hora a hora.
Os marcadores [F1], [F2]... apontam para a tabela de fontes no fim. **Checagem feita em 2026-10-11**, ver [../../pesquisa/checagem-3-roteiros-propostos.md](../../pesquisa/checagem-3-roteiros-propostos.md).

Cuidados de imagem: a guilhotina aparece de longe, nunca a execução em si (limite de violência do YouTube e da monetização). Calendário, relógio decimal e mapas desenhados em código (`cartoes.py`), como no vídeo 2.

## 0. Abertura (gancho: que dia é hoje?)

1. What's the date today?
2. Easy question. Unless you live in Paris in the spring of 1794.
3. Because for you, today is not the eighth of May.
4. Today is the nineteenth of Floréal, Year Two. [F1]
5. There are no Sundays anymore. There's no "Monsieur" or "Madame."
6. And on some official clocks, noon is "five o'clock." [F2]
7. This is Paris during the Revolution, at the height of what history calls the Terror.
8. Most videos about it talk about kings, speeches, and the guillotine.
9. Today, we'll live one ordinary day, as an ordinary Parisian.
10. Waking up. Buying bread. Going to work. Going to a meeting at night.
11. And, at the end of the day, watching one of the greatest scientists in history ride past, on his way to die.
12. But first, let me explain that date.

## 1. Um calendário novo, um mundo novo

13. In 1792, France abolished its monarchy and became a republic. [F3]
14. The revolutionaries believed they were starting history over.
15. So they decided time itself should start over too.
16. In late 1793, they adopted a new calendar. [F1]
17. Year One began on the twenty-second of September, 1792, the day after the Republic was proclaimed. [F1]
18. Every month had exactly thirty days. [F1]
19. And the months got new names, inspired by nature and the seasons.
20. Vendémiaire, the month of the grape harvest. Brumaire, the month of fog. Nivôse, the month of snow. [F1]
21. Floréal, our month, is the month of flowers. [F1]
22. And the seven-day week was gone.
23. Instead, there were ten-day weeks, called décades. [F1]
24. The days were simply called "first day," "second day," and so on, in Latin-style names, up to décadi, the tenth day. [F1]
25. Décadi was the day of rest. [F1]
26. Do the math.
27. Before, workers rested one day in seven, plus many saints' days.
28. Now, they rested one day in ten. [F1]
29. Not everyone was thrilled.
30. Each day was named after a plant. Every fifth day after an animal, and every tenth after a tool. [F1]
31. Today, the nineteenth of Floréal, is the day of the orache, a garden plant. Tomorrow, the tenth day, is the day of the hoe. [F1]
32. The revolutionaries even tried to change the clock.
33. In their decimal time, a day had ten hours, each hour a hundred minutes, each minute a hundred seconds. [F2]
34. Midnight was ten o'clock. Noon was five o'clock. [F2]
35. A few clockmakers actually built decimal clocks. Some had both dials, old and new, on the same face. [F2]
36. It never caught on. Decimal time was abandoned in 1795. [F2]
37. But one part of this project did survive.
38. The same people were busy inventing a new way to measure the world: the meter, the gram, the liter. [F4]
39. Remember that. It will matter at the end of our day.

## 2. Acordar (antes do sol)

40. Our Parisian wakes up before dawn, in a small room in the Faubourg Saint-Antoine, in the east of the city. [F5]
41. This neighborhood is famous for its furniture makers, and for its rebels. [F5]
42. Its people helped storm the Bastille, just around the corner, five years earlier. [F5]
43. Our man is a carpenter. He's in his thirties, with a wife and two kids, all in one room.
44. Paris has around six hundred thousand people. [F6]
45. Most of them live like this: crowded, in narrow streets, in old buildings several floors high.
46. There's no running water.
47. Water comes from public fountains, or from water carriers who climb the stairs with buckets, for a fee. [F7]
48. Much of it comes straight from the Seine. The same river that takes the city's waste.
49. On the front door of the building, there's a sheet of paper.
50. It lists the name of every person living inside. [F8]
51. That's the law now. The authorities want to know who lives where, and who is new in town. [F8]
52. In a few weeks, our carpenter will check that list carefully. Just to make sure nothing about it looks suspicious.

## 3. Vestir-se (a roupa como política)

53. Getting dressed in 1794 is a political act.
54. Our carpenter puts on long trousers, not the knee breeches of the rich. [F9]
55. That's why the radicals of Paris were called sans-culottes: "without breeches." [F9]
56. A short jacket. Wooden clogs, or worn shoes.
57. And on his hat, a cockade: a small rosette in blue, white and red.
58. For him, it's a proud symbol.
59. For his wife, it's also the law. Since September 1793, women in public must wear the tricolor cockade. [F10]
60. On special days, he might wear a red woolen cap, the "bonnet rouge," the cap of liberty. [F9]
61. Silk, powdered wigs, fancy buckles? Those are dangerous now.
62. They make you look like an aristocrat.
63. And looking like an aristocrat is a very bad idea in Paris, in 1794.

## 4. A fila do pão (5 da manhã)

64. Before going to work, someone in the family has to get the bread.
65. And bread is the center of life in Paris.
66. For a working family, bread could take up a huge share of the budget. [F11]
67. So when bread is scarce or expensive, Paris gets angry.
68. Many historians see bread prices as one of the sparks of the Revolution itself. [F11]
69. By 1794, the government has set maximum prices for bread and many other goods. [F12]
70. It's called the Maximum. [F12]
71. In theory, this protects the poor.
72. In practice, many merchants hide their goods, or sell them in secret, at higher prices.
73. Hoarding food has been made punishable by death. [F13]
74. And Paris has a system of bread cards, to share the bread among families. [F14]
75. So, before dawn, the lines form outside the bakeries.
76. Mostly women. Waiting, talking, complaining, gossiping.
77. These lines are where Paris gets its news.
78. Who was arrested yesterday. Which baker is cheating. What happened at the Convention.
79. Even the bread itself is political.
80. In November 1793, the Convention decreed that bakers could make only one kind of bread for everyone, rich and poor: the "bread of equality." [F14]
81. No more white bread for the wealthy and dark bread for the rest.
82. And what about coffee and sugar?
83. Since the slave revolt in the French colony of Saint-Domingue, today's Haiti, began in 1791, sugar and coffee have become scarce and expensive. [F15]
84. Parisians rioted over sugar prices in 1792. [F15]
85. So breakfast is bread, maybe soaked in a thin soup. And, if you're lucky, a little wine.

## 5. A caminho do trabalho (7 da manhã)

86. Our carpenter walks to his workshop.
87. On the way, he says hello to his neighbor.
88. Not "Bonjour, Monsieur." That's suspicious now.
89. It's "Salut, citoyen." Hello, citizen. [F16]
90. And he tries to use "tu," the informal "you," even with strangers. [F16]
91. In October 1793, a deputy asked the Convention to make "tu" the law. [F16]
92. The Convention refused, because a law like that would make anyone who slipped look suspicious. [F16]
93. But many clubs and local groups adopted it on their own. [F16]
94. He passes walls covered in posters: new laws, decrees, lists of names.
95. Newspapers are read aloud on street corners, for those who can't read.
96. The street names are changing too.
97. Anything that sounds like a king or a saint is being renamed.
98. The famous Palais-Royal is now the Palais-Égalité: the Palace of Equality. [F17]
99. Even churches have changed.
100. In late 1793, the churches of Paris were closed for worship. [F18]
101. Notre-Dame was turned into a Temple of Reason. [F18]
102. Many church bells had already been taken down and melted to make cannons. [F18]
103. So the city sounds different too. Far fewer bells.

## 6. O trabalho (de manhã)

104. The workshop is small: a master, a few journeymen, an apprentice.
105. They make chairs, tables and cabinets.
106. But fewer rich clients buy fine furniture these days.
107. Many of them have fled the country. Others are in prison.
108. So workshops take whatever work they can get.
109. And there's one huge customer: the war.
110. France is at war with most of Europe. [F19]
111. Hundreds of thousands of young men have been drafted into the army. [F19]
112. Paris workshops make guns, uniforms, shoes and wagons for them.
113. Even the soil is drafted.
114. Citizens are asked to scrape the walls of cellars and stables, to collect saltpeter, an ingredient for gunpowder. [F20]
115. The pay is in paper money, called assignats. [F21]
116. And every month, the assignat buys less. [F21]
117. Our carpenter knows this well.
118. His wage seems fine on paper. At the bakery, it's a different story.

## 7. Almoço e o "quanto você vale" (meio-dia)

119. At midday, the work stops for a meal.
120. Bread, of course. Maybe some vegetables, cheese, or a soup.
121. Meat is rare and expensive for a family like this.
122. At the table, the workers talk. But carefully.
123. Because in 1794, a careless word can be dangerous.
124. In September 1793, the Convention passed the Law of Suspects. [F22]
125. It allowed the arrest of anyone who, by their conduct, their words or their writings, showed themselves to be an enemy of liberty. [F22]
126. That is a very wide net.
127. To keep a job, to get a passport, or just to prove you're a good citizen, you might need a certificate of civism. [F23]
128. A piece of paper, issued by your local section, saying that you are a loyal patriot. [F23]
129. No certificate? You become a suspect.
130. So people watch what they say.
131. Neighbors watch neighbors.
132. Old disputes with a landlord, a rival or an ex-partner can suddenly become denunciations.

## 8. A tarde: o que mudou para as mulheres

133. While our carpenter works, his wife works too.
134. Maybe she sells vegetables at a market, sews for a shop, or washes clothes in the Seine.
135. The Revolution has changed some things for her.
136. Since 1792, French couples can get divorced. And many women in Paris have used that right. [F24]
137. Women march, sign petitions, and fill the public galleries of the political clubs.
138. But they can't vote. [F25]
139. In October 1793, the Convention banned women's political clubs. [F25]
140. A few days later, the writer Olympe de Gouges, who had published a Declaration of the Rights of Woman, was guillotined. [F25]
141. The Revolution talked a lot about equality.
142. It didn't extend it to everyone.

## 9. O fim da tarde: as carroças

143. In the late afternoon, there's a sound in the Rue Saint-Honoré.
144. Wooden wheels on stone.
145. Carts are coming from the Conciergerie, the prison next to the court of the Revolutionary Tribunal. [F26]
146. They're carrying people who were sentenced to death earlier today.
147. They roll along the Rue Saint-Honoré, toward the Place de la Révolution. [F26]
148. Today, we call it the Place de la Concorde.
149. On the way, they pass very close to a house where a lawyer named Maximilien Robespierre rents a room. [F27]
150. He's one of the most powerful men in France right now.
151. In less than three months, he'll make the same trip. [F28]
152. And today, there are many carts.
153. Twenty-eight tax farmers will die today.
154. They belonged to a company that collected taxes for the old king, called the General Farm. [F29]
155. Ordinary Parisians hated the tax farmers. Many people won't feel sorry for them.
156. But one of the men in the carts is not ordinary at all.

## 10. Lavoisier (o homem que mediu o mundo)

157. His name is Antoine Lavoisier. [F29]
158. He is fifty years old. [F29]
159. And he is one of the greatest scientists who ever lived.
160. Lavoisier showed that, in a chemical reaction, matter is not created or destroyed. It only changes form. [F30]
161. He named oxygen and hydrogen. [F30]
162. He helped explain what fire really is, and what happens when we breathe. [F30]
163. Many people call him the father of modern chemistry.
164. And he worked on a commission that was creating the metric system. The meter, the kilogram. [F4]
165. The same system that most of the world uses today.
166. Until December 1793, when the government purged him from that commission, for political reasons. [F4]
167. But Lavoisier made one big mistake.
168. To fund his science, he had invested in the General Farm. [F29]
169. That made him a tax collector, in the eyes of the Revolution.
170. Next to him in the carts is his father-in-law, Jacques Paulze. [F29]
171. They will die on the same day.
172. His wife, Marie-Anne Paulze Lavoisier, was his partner in the laboratory. [F31]
173. She drew his instruments, translated scientific works, and kept his notes. [F31]
174. Today, she loses her husband and her father.
175. There's a famous story about the trial.
176. When someone asked the court to spare a great scientist, the judge supposedly answered: "The Republic has no need of scientists." [F32]
177. It's a great line. But it's an anecdote, and it may never have been said. [F32]
178. The real quote everyone remembers came later, from the mathematician Joseph-Louis Lagrange. [F33]
179. "It took them only an instant to cut off that head, and a hundred years may not be enough to produce another like it." [F33]
180. The guillotine stands in the middle of the square.
181. There's a crowd. Some people come every day. Others just walk past without looking.
182. That's perhaps the most disturbing part of the Terror.
183. It became part of the routine.
184. Between the spring of 1793 and the summer of 1794, the Revolutionary Tribunal in Paris sentenced more than two thousand six hundred people to death. [F34]
185. Across France, the official death sentences reached about seventeen thousand. [F34]
186. And many more died in prisons, or without any trial at all. [F34]

## 11. A noite: a assembleia da seção

187. After dinner, our carpenter doesn't go to bed.
188. He goes to a meeting.
189. Paris is divided into forty-eight neighborhoods, called sections. [F35]
190. Each one has its own assembly, where citizens debate, vote, and denounce. [F35]
191. The meetings happen in old churches and convents.
192. To make sure poor workers could attend, the Convention paid poorer citizens forty sous to take part. [F35]
193. Tonight, the topics are the usual ones.
194. The price of bread. The war. Someone's certificate of civism. A neighbor who said the wrong thing.
195. The meeting is loud, smoky, and long.
196. And it's also, in a strange way, exciting.
197. A carpenter is debating the future of his country, in a room where, a few years ago, only priests and nobles had a voice.
198. That's the contradiction of 1794.
199. Ordinary people had more political power than ever before.
200. And they were more afraid than ever before.

## 12. Fechamento: o fim do Terror

201. Late at night, our carpenter walks home, under the dim oil lanterns that hang over the streets.
202. He climbs the stairs. Checks on the kids. Goes to sleep.
203. Tomorrow is the twentieth of Floréal: décadi, the day of rest.
204. He doesn't know it yet, but the Terror is almost over.
205. In June, it gets even worse. In about seven weeks, between the Law of 22 Prairial and Robespierre's fall, the Paris tribunal sentences one thousand three hundred and seventy-six people to death. [F34]
206. In the thirteen months before that, it had sentenced about twelve hundred. [F34]
207. And then, on the ninth of Thermidor, Year Two, the twenty-seventh of July 1794, the Convention turns on Robespierre. [F28]
208. The next day, he rides in a cart down the Rue Saint-Honoré, to the guillotine. [F28]
209. Within a few years, most of the revolutionary experiments disappear.
210. Decimal time is gone. The calendar lasts until Napoleon ends it, at the start of 1806. [F1]
211. The seven-day week comes back. And so do Monsieur and Madame.
212. But one thing survives.
213. The meter. The kilogram. The liter.
214. The system Lavoisier helped create is now used by almost every country on Earth. [F4]
215. Every time you buy a kilo of rice, or run a hundred meters, you're using a little piece of the French Revolution.
216. Built, in part, by a man the Revolution killed.
217. If you enjoyed this day in Paris, tell me in the comments: which day in history should we live next?
